## The 13 ways (in his order)

### 1. Network effects (~00:00–03:00)
- [S] Two types: (a) **customer base** — a large user base attracts more users; each added user makes the network more valuable (Facebook; telecoms' free in-network texting and family plans). (b) **product ecosystem** — closed loop where products work only with each other (Apple: one champion product pulls in products with less affinity).
- [S] Diagnostic question: "is there a way I can structure what I have so that if more people enter my network from a customer base it adds value to every other customer?" [S] Warning: "the opposite of this is a traditional service business: one-on-one provides value, one-on-two provides less; most businesses are structured to deteriorate as more customers come in." Counter-example: a dentist buying group gains buying power with each member.
- [S] Tech companies lose billions to establish networks because once mapped they profit many ways.

### 2. Exclusive control over a resource or technology (~03:00–06:00)
- [S] Physical resources (De Beers controls mines and distribution → sets prices; "more diamonds than dishwashers in the US") or **data and proprietary tech** (patents, unreplicable data). [E] Google search: 20 years of user data → better models; his bet: "it's the best AI that will win, not necessarily the first one, because as soon as you use a better one you'll immediately switch."

### 3. Government regulation and licensing (~06:00–09:00)
- [S] Government grants (Amtrak, Con Edison ~$16B revenue) and strict licensing (casino licence "a licence to print money", lotteries). [E] Family story: a relative owned Iran's national lottery pre-revolution. [S] Small-scale version: knowing how to navigate regulation (a liquor licence → high-margin profit centre).

### 4. Economies of scale (~09:00–11:00)
- [S] Lower cost per unit through volume → can undercut and deter smaller entrants; a **low-cost-leader strategy**. [S] Cautionary quote (Dan Kennedy): "there's no advantage to being the second cheapest player in the marketplace." [S] Advantage "gets better with time."
- [E] ALAN: 100 → 1 000 → 4–5 000 appointments/day; sends millions of messages/day → lower cost per message than a later entrant, "increasing margins" plus data no one else has: "a compounding advantage… if someone starts two years later."

### 5. Vertical integration (~11:00–14:30)
- [S] "Sweetheart of private equity" (capital can do it); control over **supply** and over **distribution**, "click to close". Benefits: capture margin at every step; control quality; control disruption/risk from vendors going out of business ("control is the opposite of risk"). [E] Tesla; De Beers; his self-publishing (owns customer relationship, writes the book, prints and distributes; could next own warehouses → presses → paper mills → forests). [S] "Everything in between is where all the middlemen take a slice."

### 6. Strong brand identity and customer loyalty (~14:30–16:30) — "my personal favorite"
- [S] Why favorite: others need huge time, complexity or capital; brand "you can make with skill"; anyone can build one. Brand becomes synonymous with the product (Google, Kleenex, Band-Aid). Result: put the logo on a commoditized product → higher conversion, lower cost per customer, premium prices (Nike). "If you can price above your competition and increase the demand… what a competitive advantage."
- [S] How: "keep their promises and make associations clearly and deliberately over a long period of time with what their customers value, and strongly disassociate with the things and people their customers don't value, until people associate that value intrinsically with the brand itself." (He points to a separate keynote on brand.)

### 7. Strategic pricing (~16:30–20:30)
- [S] **Predatory pricing** (his words, "don't read into it"): temporarily low prices where your profits outlast selling at a loss so no one can enter; in software = feature wars, copy the competitor's feature and bundle it at no extra price (Microsoft Office; Instagram Stories stalling Snapchat).
- [S] **Price discrimination**: selling the same thing at different prices to different people by spending power/need (airlines using card data; airlines' real income is ~3 % of card spend). [S] Small-business translation: "be very clear on the one or two customer segments or avatars you really serve and be able to segment those people so you can sell to the right wallet."
- [S] His ethical rule: he does not sell literally the same thing at two prices; he **tweaks the offer slightly** (add "a free cocktail") so the two offers are different and he can defend it if the two buyers compare. "This especially works well with services." [S] Pricing is "one of the easiest levers to build and manipulate", faster and smaller-scale than regulation or economies of scale.

### 8. Exclusive contracts (~20:30–24:30)
- [S] Three parties: **supply** (Hershey's with cocoa farmers → rivals must resell from you), **customers** (government contracts, Lockheed, Oracle: incumbency compounds over years), **distribution** (exclusive retail; Coca-Cola/Pepsi drop acquired brands into every restaurant/7-Eleven/Walmart; "they don't even have to innovate, just wait for the market to say this is a winner", pay the founder $500M, make it worth $5B). Physical-world distribution is especially hard to replicate.
- [S] Condition: "fundamentally a B2B or B2B2C strategy." [S] For a smaller business to win distribution: "make it worth it for the distributor" — lower cost, a retail kit that moves product, train their staff for free: "add other perks and incentives that more established players probably aren't willing to do or don't need to do."

### 9. Huge capital requirements (~24:30–26:30)
- [S] Capital-intensive entry knocks out 99.9 % of owners (SpaceX/Starlink, oil, pharma with years of loss then "print money for 30 years"). [S] Small-scale: a social media agency needs only skill; a manufacturing plant or capital equipment business needs capital → if you can raise or have saved capital, "pick vehicles… that have fewer people competing against you; you automatically shrink the pool."

### 10. Intellectual property (~26:30–30:30)
- [S] Patents/copyright/trademarks (book contents, Mickey Mouse expiring) vs **trade secrets** (hard to enforce, employees leave; Coca-Cola recipe; Monster reportedly paid ~$500M for its own recipe — "fact check me"). [S] Warning: "someone's using my offer, my ad, my content — that's not considered intellectual property." Test word: **pretending** (can't pretend to be acquisition.com or to have written the book). [E] He estimates his book rights could sell for ~$100M off its revenue.
- [S] **Royalty/licensing play:** licence 10 big brands (Oreo: 8 % of revenue) to "piggyback on 20 years of branding"; [E] acquaintance built a supplement line on Swedish Fish / Sour Patch Kids flavours.

### 11. Acquisitions and mergers (~30:30–35:00)
- [S] "You kind of just assimilate your competition"; PE darling; sits "on top of any of the others" (acquire the supply chain, competitors for economies of scale, distribution/contracts). [E] Meta buying Instagram/WhatsApp; Waste Management built by buying routes in a fragmented market.
- [E] Roofing company (~$60M/yr, biggest buyer of the biggest US lead source, can't scale beyond it): "buy it or build it" — his recommendation: buy 1–3 small agencies that sell pay-per-lead to roofers; next buy the financing company that finances the roofs.
- [S] "Small businesses get small and stay small through competition; big businesses think about consolidation." Five neck-and-neck local owners: one sells to PE, PE buys the rest. "I'd rather own 20 % of a billion-dollar company than 100 % of a million-dollar company." Home-maintenance businesses (lawn care, pool cleaning): consolidate one city → economies of scale, denser routes, relationships; "huge private equity interest… tons of M&A."
- [S] Lens: "don't think from the competitive lens, think: how do I eat up things that would cause risk to my business" (distribution, customer bases, media assets).

### 12. Innovative business model (~35:00–39:30) — "one of my favorites"
- [S] His saying: "find what everyone wants, make it better, give it away for free, and step four: monetize another way." Freemium as a marketing strategy: effort goes into ascending free → paid.
- [S] **Equation:** cost to acquire a customer = cost to deliver the free thing ÷ conversion rate (1 cent to service a free user, 1 in 100 convert → $1 CAC). "Take what would normally be a marketing budget and put it into delivering the free thing… so good that word of mouth generates more demand." (Google Docs vs Microsoft; Spotify ads vs premium.)
- [E] Smaller-scale tests (his own): Prestige Meals/Prestige Labs test kitchen: "free gym forever" if on the meals (didn't work; "a bit of a distraction… trying to innovate this business model"). [S] Logic: once the capital cost of the gym is incurred, marginal users cost little; give away what others charge for if they buy the higher-profit stream. "Even if you go from $20 to zero it's still free and free gets 10× the interest of not free."
- [E] Hypothetical he "would test": free gym use under rules (can kick out riff-raff) → mandatory onboarding consultation where personal training is sold; fine if only 1 in 5 buy "because I didn't pay for the customers", as long as PT profit covers facility cost.

### 13. Control over distribution channels (~39:30–41:30)
- [S] Stronger than contracts because contracts can be broken; ownership lets you "profit from everyone else's business too" (Amazon: Prime, warehouses, own delivery; sellers pay a large share of revenue). [S] Small-scale: **local distribution** — dominate a small area and expand outward ("in that tiny area you're the best"); or **distribution on a specific segment** ("if I know every CEO in America on a first-name basis… I can sell private jets"). "Means of communicating or delivering messages or products to customers that you have complete control over."

### Zoom-out (~41:30–42:30)
- [S] Monopoly = only one can sell, via supply, demand, media that talks to those people, technology, capital, or licence. "You can create life-changing wealth and not become a billionaire — create a small network of a very small niche that you own."
