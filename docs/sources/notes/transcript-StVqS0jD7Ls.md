### 1. Sales multiplier 1 — sell seven days a week (00:00:08–00:03:12, "sell 7 days per week")
- [S] Rule: sell 7 days/week, including weekends and evenings, because in many industries prospects are only available off-hours (from ~5 pm) — especially retail and anything selling to consumers with normal jobs.
- [E] In fitness he closed all his sales 4–8 pm; "no one's going to buy" 9–5.
- [S] Math: 52 weeks × 2 extra days = 104 extra selling days/year = 29% increase over 5-day selling → "minimum 29%, sometimes more".
- [S] Mechanism of the "stacked Monday": if Sat/Sun are unavailable, weekend bookings pile onto Monday and then no-show/reschedule, because the prospect wanted to come when they clicked the ad (Sat/Sun).
- [E] Across his portfolio companies, more people schedule on Saturday and Sunday (for Monday) than on other days — i.e. they are available right then.
- [S] Second-order effect: pulling Monday appointments back to Sat/Sun gives same-day/next-day appointments, which have higher show-up rates → multiplier across the business.

### 2. Sales multiplier 2 — respond to leads in under one minute (00:03:12–00:07:04)
- [S] Stat cited: contacting a lead within 60 seconds of opting in → 391% increase in likelihood of closing — "you could 4X your business tomorrow" just by installing sub-60-second contact processes.
- [S] Decision rule: ask of every other activity "does it have an immediate 4X?" If not, stop and do this instead.
- [S] 50% of prospects go with the business that responds first, not the best one. [E] Leila calling salons out of town: first two said "appointment only", the third took her immediately and got the business.
- [S] "People have huge motivation for a tiny period of time" — open the door in that window.
- [S] If response > 5 minutes, close likelihood drops by 80% ("four out of five times you would have closed now goes to 20%"). Therefore "lead quality going down" is usually a lead-working problem, not a lead problem.
- [S] Follow-up should be personal, fast, multiple times over multiple days — but the first follow-up (speed of acknowledgement) matters most; customers judge how "buttoned up" you are by response speed.
- [E] Data sources for the whole video: (a) a Harvard Business Review study of sales metrics across industries; (b) ALAN, his software company doing 4,000+ appointments/day, where they could see click-to-close. Finding: opening up calendars was "the single strongest correlate to overall throughput" — more than raising close rates 10–20%, because you cannot get 400% increases from closing skill but can from these outside-the-call multipliers.
- [S] Principle: things outside the sale affect sales more than anything inside the sale.

### 3. Sales multiplier 3 — "call now" option (00:07:04–00:08:06)
- [S] On the funnel's scheduling step add a "call now" option; intent "could not be stronger".
- [S] Condition: if you must qualify leads, put "call now" after qualification.
- [S] Capacity rule: if adding one sales rep can 2–3x revenue and the relative revenue increase is significantly greater than the onboarding cost, hire the rep so prospects can call in.

### 4. Sales multiplier 4 — 15-minute time slots and the off-the-call SOP (00:08:06–00:11:43)
- [E] At ALAN, changing how times appeared on the calendar raised schedule rate and show-up rate: 15-minute start increments instead of 1-hour slots. You don't sell in 15 minutes; the booking consumes the next three blocks.
- [S] Two fixes for awkward gaps between calls: (1) hire more reps, (2) an "off-the-call SOP" — step-by-step for what reps do when not on a call (most managers only write the on-the-call SOP, which is why reps are unproductive).
- [S] Warning: faking a busy calendar (blocking out slots to look in demand) "doesn't do anything… it's dumb" — tested; it only makes booking less convenient.
- [S] Off-the-call SOP content = "pulling up appointments": reps call new leads (the Saturday-books-Monday people), qualify them (which you should do anyway for anything taking a full sales slot), and offer "I had an opening later today, do you want the slot?" — turns a 3-day-out appointment into a same-day one.
- [S] Even if the lead can't move up, they were contacted within minutes, know you're legit, and are pre-qualified. Effects: higher rep utilisation (stacked calendar, fewer gaps), higher booking rates, higher show rates, plus the freed future slot becomes available to someone else.

### 5. Sales multiplier 5 — "feed the killers" (00:11:43–00:14:58)

- [S] "I feel bad for the weak reps" — it's a business decision; if your worst reps churn out, why worry.
- [S] Side effects: (a) a top rep earning $500k–$3M attracts people who will work below market wage for the shot at being top dog; (b) the bottom tier is a "churn factory" where newcomers learn on the hardest leads and qualify themselves as worthy of better leads.

### 6. Sales multiplier 6 — reward show-up rates, not just close rates (00:14:58–00:16:30)
- [S] Getting a whole team +30% in close rate is hard "unless you just absolutely suck"; show-up rates usually have large slack.
- [S] Weekly kudos/shout-outs to closers with the highest show-up rates (not just highest close rates); ask what they did (answer is usually "I followed the process").
- [S] Optional: flat prize, e.g. $500/week for highest show-up rate — "material enough that it's worth showing off for".
- [S] Reasoning: +20% close rate and +20% show rate are the same revenue; if reps close at 35%, +20% is very hard, whereas reminding them to work leads and confirm appointments is easy. Claim: this can raise sales "more than sales training can sometimes".

### 7. Sales multiplier 7 — the reminder sequence (00:16:30–00:19:07)
- [S] Automated reminders are mandatory, and should look automated — "don't try and pretend that automation is a real person, everyone knows".
- [S] Layer manual reminders on top (needs far less volume to work). Three manual touches from ALAN data with the highest show-up rates: (1) 24 hours before, (2) morning of, (3) 1 hour before.
- [S] The 24-hour touch must show prep/personalisation so the prospect feels worse about not showing. Examples: weight-loss — "I set aside a t-shirt for you, what size/colour?"; B2B — "I looked over your site, talked to my team, we have three things we can immediately help with."
- [S] Claim: 5 minutes of prep per prospect → ~30% higher show-up rate; "in 60 minutes a day you can increase total sales throughput by 30%".
- [S] Channel preference: "blue messages" (iMessage) over green SMS because people don't believe SMS is a real person; voice memos and video work well for reminders.
- [S] Script example (24h): "Hey John, really excited to meet tomorrow, we outlined six things for your business… pulled from my team and from looking at the site… we're at 4:00 tomorrow, hit me up if there's any issues, otherwise see you then."

### 9. Sales training — why train at all (00:19:57–00:21:12)
- [S] Most sales orgs don't know how to train and "just run churn factories" (bring 100 in, see who survives). That's fine, but if you can train, you can compensate below market because you make mediocre people good rather than paying for existing skill — the owner's "arbitrage opportunity".
- [S] Salespeople should still earn a lot; it's a marketplace — a 10× bigger pool to recruit from is an advantage.

### 10. Daily huddles and role-play with fast feedback (00:21:12–00:25:03)
- [S] Role-playing is "probably the single greatest skill you need to learn in sales" (as a trainer).
- [S] Warning: managers/entrepreneurs avoid role-play from fear of looking bad. Set the frame: "no one's perfect, that's why we practice, I'll mess up, you'll catch me." Any ego reduces the amount of practice.
- [S] How NOT to do it: rep runs the whole script, then after 20–30 minutes gets a list of corrections. Two problems: (1) people can only take one piece of feedback at a time; (2) trainability is inversely correlated with feedback delay — the faster the feedback (1 s vs 2 s vs 3 s), the fewer repetitions needed ("across species").
- [S] Method: announce the one thing being worked on; give real-time positive reinforcement (nods, thumbs up, "great, keep going"); on a miss, pause: "say it like this, try again" — then repeat the correct version 2–6 times in quick succession so a "mini recorder" plays on the real call.
- [S] Pre-frame: "I'll probably stop you 30 or 40 times during this one script, that's expected, it means we're doing it right."
- [S] Cadence: one skill can be the focus for a week or two. Daily huddle = team-level issue drilled together; one-on-ones = one thing specific to that person.

- [S] "Good closers try and get hot; great closers never get cold" — because they've systematised every item of the "monster checklist" that creates hotness. Being hot proves you can close at that rate.

### 16. Slumps: read testimonials, kudos for conviction (00:34:22–00:37:40)

### 21. Favourite local-lead call script (00:43:45–00:46:49)
- [S] Don't open with "Hi John, this is Alex, when do you want to come in?" — they showed interest but don't know who you are.
- [S] Script with tonality: "John?" (tone goes up; pause) → "yeah, who's this?" → "This is Alex." (tone implies he should know you; pause; you can hear them thinking) → "I'm calling because you opted in on Facebook for our promotion, and I just wanted to make sure that one, you weren't a crazy person on the internet (ha), and if there was a time that worked for you to come in — and obviously to make sure you knew we weren't crazy people either." Steamroll through; give three mental cues (Facebook, opt-in, specific promotion) because people don't remember what they opted in for — especially if you call two days later ("they're in a different universe… might have opted in for 10 things").

### 25. Follow the script; tone, emphasis, pacing; tone-guided scripts (00:53:13–00:57:55)
- [S] Script adherence is the first thing to drill. He would rather have 100% of the team word-perfect and closing no one — then he has a script problem he can fix centrally; if reps improvise, nothing can be changed except "onesie-twosie".
- [S] Scope: the script runs up to the ask; after the ask come the loops/overcomes you drill separately. "The first two-thirds of the sale should be pretty much word for word the same"; prospect answers change, scripting doesn't.
- [S] Levels of adherence: (1) saying the words; (2) how they're said — pacing (speed up approaching the close, slow down and lower voice at the ask), emphasis (the "I didn't say he hit his wife" exercise, credited to Jason F[ladlien?] — same words, seven meanings depending on stress), tone (rising tone turns "you're doing 3 million a year" into a question that demands a response).
- [S] Implementation: write the script with a tone guide using word-processor formatting — underline = slow down, ALL CAPS = emphasise, italics = create space, bold = fourth cue. Four cues for the closer.
- [S] Size: a script is ~12 questions with one or two follow-ups each, not the Gettysburg Address; the team should be able to "breathe the script" quickly.

### 26. The CLOSER framework (00:57:55–01:13:00)
- [S] Scope condition: CLOSER is for transactional sales — one-call or two-call closes. For more complex sales he has a separate "diagnostic script" (referenced, made a separate video). He says the acronym isn't magic; it works because people remember it and therefore change behaviour.
- **C — Clarify why they're here** (00:58:27): nobody gets on a call "for more information"; they want to solve a problem. Questions: "why did you take the time to hop on today / what made you reach out / opt in?" These remind them of the action they took. Even outbound leads have taken one action (commented, replied, booked, kept listening) — remind them and tie it to a problem.
- **L — Label them with the problem** (00:59:30): shortest but critical: "so just to be clear, you're here, you want to get there, and this has been holding you back." Establishes the gap between present and desired (plus the perceived obstacle). "Now you've set the table."
- **O — Overview past pain / the pain cycle** (01:00:10): "deprivation creates motivation" (hungry → eat; tired → sleep). The call should bring their deprivation top of mind and expand it → motivation to act. Political-poll analogy: the topic chosen to deprive people of becomes the election issue. Team ism: "the pain is the pitch." Quantify what they've spent in time/effort, the status impact, and the cost of inaction per day.
  - [E] Weight-loss pain cycle: "what have you done so far to lose weight?" Whatever they answer (Weight Watchers, a personal trainer, a gym) maps onto his three-pillar pitch: Weight Watchers = nutrition + accountability but no fitness ("you lost the weight and gained it back because you lost muscle"); a gym = fitness, maybe nutrition, no accountability. "Everything they've done is an advantage for you because they are here, which means it didn't work." Personal-trainer prospect: "he's obviously not doing a good job — you're here."
  - [S] Rule: highlight the good in what they tried and name the missing pillar; "all we have to do is fix this one thing".
- **Three-pillar pitch** (01:03:57): every business has one buried in it. [E] Script for a marketing company selling leads to mortgage brokers/realtors: leads must be timely, qualified, exclusive — essentially BANT with need already established by the lead. Pain cycle: "ever bought leads before?" → "Zillow leads weren't exclusive, you competed with 20 guys" → "if they were exclusive could you make money?" → gap established.
  - [S] Not always literally three, but "humans tend to think in threes" — chunk a seven-step process up to three; nutrition could be split into calories/macros/micros/timing/composition but don't.
- **S — Sell the vacation** (01:05:57): talk about Maui, not the flight or TSA — the experience after the transformation. For each pillar: make the statement of what they need, then give a metaphor. Most teams train product, not prospect.
  - [S] Metaphors he used: (a) three legs of the stool (two legs = balances briefly then falls — "lost 50, gained back 60"); (b) toothbrush (credited to Alison Prince): kids hate brushing, parents provide external accountability, positive reinforcement becomes internal habit — "we'll artificially be that external motivation until the habit forms" — note he sells the concept, not response times/channels; (c) The Amazing Race (credited to Tom Miller): the couple that won too often paid a local to sit in the back seat and navigate; "I've been where you're going, with a hundred others like you, I can be your backseat driver — unfair advantage"; (d) SEO vs ads: ads = paycheck (work in, money out immediately), SEO = investment account (compounds, bigger than anything active in 1–2 years).

### 27. Closing — preface: train back to front; closing begins at "no" (01:13:00–01:16:40)
- [S] Train sales back to front: first teach how to collect and process payment (without it, 0% close chance), then how to ask for payment / to get started, then the top of the script.
- [S] "Closing begins once someone says no." One no isn't forever; train for and expect no — "that is the job of sales". If everyone said yes you'd just need a video and a buy button (Tesla does this); depending on marketing quality you may not need sales at all.
- [S] The one time to shut up: when they say yes. Beginners keep talking, add details, and turn a yes into a maybe/no. On agreement: stop talking, ask for payment, close.
- [S] "Prep = smooth": in one breath you should know what you'll say to any objection ("think about it", "give me a day", "talk to my spouse", "don't like this feature"). Anything that surprises you means you're underprepared — you have this one conversation 10 times a day for a living. Don't sound canned: use AAA (acknowledge, associate, ask) to make it natural.

### 28. Rapid-fire stall questions before any "real" closing (01:16:40–01:19:55)
- [S] Ordered list of questions after "I need to think about it":
  1. Early in the sale: "What's your main concern?" → back into the sale.
  2. Later in the sale, harder: "What are you afraid of having happen?" / "What's the big thing you're trying to avoid?" — surfaces the real obstacle (which you can overcome) vs "think about it" (which you can't).
  3. "What are the variables you're using to make the decision?"
  4. "What makes this a yes?" — if they can't answer, "can I share the decision-making variables I often hear?"
  5. "What makes this a no?" — people are afraid to ask this; you've had this conversation more times than they have.
- [S] AAA example on "I don't know": acknowledge ("that's amazing"), associate ("anyone deciding this fast would be crazy, we wouldn't want to work with them… it sounds like you're just nervous, which makes you like all the people who buy and get amazing results — it shows you care; if you care you'll follow through"), ask ("ready to rock and roll?" — tone shifts up).

### 29. All-purpose closes (01:19:55–01:22:26)
- [S] All-purpose closes = the 80/20 of closes; they work for any objection because they chunk up out of the weeds. You still need specific overcomes for price, stall, decision-maker and preference objections.
- [S] Close 1: "Do you think the reason you're on the phone today is because you've struggled to make this decision in the past?" Variants: money → "do you think the fact that you haven't invested in the past is the reason you're not successful?"; spouse → "do you think the reason you haven't been successful is because you've always needed permission?" Formula: take their objection + "do you think that's the reason you haven't succeeded?" — leverage: they're on the phone with the pain and haven't succeeded yet.
- [S] Close 2: 1-to-10 close: "on a scale of 1–10, 10 = let's do this, 1 = never talk to me again — where are we?" Whatever they say: "what would make it a 10?" If they say six: "why not a one?" — they list your strengths in their own words. Then: "I must have communicated poorly — we take care of that." Then: "are we feeling tennish?"

### 30. Details are death traps (01:22:26–01:28:17)
- [S] Why deals die in details: you don't get to pick whether your answer is right. Answering "how many days a week are you open?" directly is a "blind answer" (dice roll). Rule: ask a question about their question — "how many days were you looking for?" — then hedge/frame the answer. Like a cross-examining lawyer: never answer a question you don't already know the answer to.
- [S] If they press: appeal to the authority of the business. "Secretary close": "it's like asking the heart surgeon's secretary what's wrong with your heart before seeing the surgeon." "Mechanic close": "I can't ethically answer until we bring the car into the shop and look under the hood… and if anyone gives you an answer before looking, run the other way, they're selling not helping" → moral high ground, zoom out. He used it for "what diet will you give me?" — "it depends".
- [S] Theory: a detail obstacle is a preference obstacle — "I want your results but my way; you can't have both." Detail close: "change the variables, change the outcome… you told me what you've tried hasn't worked (pain cycle)… if you follow our process I know the numbers we can do — does that sound fair?" (lower tone, sound reasonable). Gym example: "can I eat what I'm eating? No — eating what you're eating got you the body you've got." Business owner: "you've got to change to change."
- [S] First response to any detail question = ask why they care / specifics: "which certifications are you looking for?" → "I don't know" → "sounds like you want an informed decision. What I can tell you is [each trainer certified three ways, 1,000+ sessions]… I'm guessing you're asking because you want to be safe — fair? Not a single injury in two years. Better? Great." ("What I can tell you is" became muscle memory.) Support-email example: "how many questions were you looking for?… the reason behind the reason is you want to fully understand; if you fully understood would you have questions? No. Then that's what we'll make sure of — ready?"
- [S] Decision rule: question the detail, or guess the reason behind it and chunk up and close, or mechanic/secretary close. Never live in the details — (1) not your expertise, (2) they get to tell you what's right.

### 31. The AAA system and the law of asking (01:28:17–01:31:45)
- [S] AAA = what separated his top reps from mediocre ones (they did it naturally; he only spotted it years in). (1) Acknowledge — repeat their last phrase back; buys thinking time and builds rapport (active listening). (2) Associate — with something positive/neutral ("smart question", "totally understandable") or with a foil: someone you talked to earlier today who bought and succeeded, or an authority in the business ("our senior engineer told me this — can I share it?"). Foils let you say a harsh truth without attacking their character ("I'm telling you what I told them"). (3) Ask the next question to move forward.
- [S] Law: "He who asks the most times gets the most sales." Asking wrong loses sales (they stop wanting to talk to you, get in a bad mood, don't buy). Rapport is what reconciles the two — it gives "unlimited shots on goal"; when their demeanour closes off you've already lost. AAA lets you deliver harsher overcomes that move the sale 2–3 steps.

### 32. Childlike curiosity; questions control; be like smoke (01:31:45–01:37:00)
- [S] Team reminder "childlike curiosity": seek to understand, not win. On a non-buy, first instinct should be "huh, interesting, tell me more", not a hard close. Isms: "be right or be rich"; you become rich by making them right — they believe nothing you say and everything they say, hence questions.
- [S] "The person asking is the person closing" — questions guide the conversation. Sales as teaching: "same conditions, new behaviour" (buying), motivated by deprivation and bridged by the solution.
- [S] When they ask a question it's a hot potato — ask back to regain control (details are death because they are asking, i.e. in control). "No one can disagree with a question" → be like smoke; statements are bombs. Never disagree with prospects (you can agree with the reason behind the question, or that it's understandable) — not the same as agreeing with everything.
- [S] Check what answer they want, then say it: "what kind of food's on the plan?" → "what kind are you looking for?"; "what are the workouts like?" → "what are you looking for?" → "cardio and weights" → "that's exactly what we do." Ethics: it's about framing, not deceiving — if they want cardio and you have none: "why?" → "to lose weight" → "if we could get you the weight loss without cardio, would you still want it?… do you care a lot about how we get there?" → most say no.
- [S] Decision tree: detail question → ask what the right answer is → if you have it, "great, that's what we have"; if not, ask why → chunk up to the reason → "if we got to the same result, would you care how?" Never answer a question you don't know the answer to.

### 33. Kill zombies early; same side of the table; when in doubt say it back (01:37:00–01:39:50)
- [S] "Killing zombies early / defuse the bomb before the close": zombies are the BANT items. Ask "anyone else who needs to be involved in this decision?"; labelling gives need; timing "good to start now?"; budget via the application/qualifier — and best of all inside the pain cycle: what they did before reveals need, timing, budget, "was your husband involved in that decision?"
- [S] Rule: whatever consistently blows up in the close, load it earlier, before asking for money — there are no stakes on the table yet. After money is asked, the same questions feel like extraction.
- [S] Positioning: same side of the table (literally in person; figuratively on Zoom/phone — corner of the table, shoulder to shoulder); early in the sale be a fact-finder and helpful guide.
- [S] Ism: "when in doubt, say it back" — slowly restate their question to buy time and keep rapport instead of an awkward silence.

### 34. Before getting real, get permission (01:39:50–01:41:52)
- [S] Frame-dependent. If you're seen as an authority: "can I be a coach instead of a friend?", "can I put my real hat on?", "mind if I get real with you?" Condition where it fails: a 20-year-old closing 40-year-old owners doing $20M/year — then use foils instead: someone just like you, someone like you who succeeded, or an authority in the business/product (senior engineer, founder) whose authority the rep transfers. "Would it be totally overstepping if I shared something uncomfortable?"; "can I tell you what I told someone in your exact position?"
- [S] Condition: consumer weight-loss coaching → the coach/friend lines are natural (you're starting a coaching relationship). If you're "just a salesman", stick with the foils.
- [S] If a close feels unsayable, it's because you didn't AAA before it.

### 35. Stack closes (01:41:52–01:44:14)
- [S] When they're on the fence, "right hook, jab, jab" — stack 2–3 closes from different angles on the same obstacle. He does not fully subscribe to "whoever talks first after the price loses" (maybe on the price drop itself), otherwise fill the pause with more closes.
- [E] "I don't have a lot of time" → three angles: (1) "first thing we do is cut 90% of what isn't working so you spend time on what matters"; (2) "it's priorities not time — others in your industry with the same resources grow faster"; (3) the seasons trap: "will you ever not be busy again? … ever be busy again? Then if you can't succeed while busy you'll fall off when busy again — for this to be permanent we make it work in all seasons, fair?" Then weave back to the others.
- [S] Knowing the theory behind a close beats memorising closes.

### 36. Don't memorise — understand the fallacy behind each objection category (01:44:14–01:47:23)
- [S] He hasn't memorised closes word for word; knows the gist. Categories and the underlying argument:
  - Time / too busy → prioritisation; reallocate resources.
  - Decision maker → "support not permission"; empower them; rely on the partner's past behaviour; fact-find; "better to ask forgiveness than permission".
  - Details → preferences ("your thing my way"); change the variables, change the outcome.
  - Money → value: "nothing is ever too expensive, it's just not worth it" (a Ferrari for $5k — you'd find the money); second layer: resourcefulness not resources — you'd call 10 friends; so they don't yet see the value.
  - Stalls → decision avoidance: either fear of the wrong decision (reassure) or not knowing how to decide (walk them through a logical decision).
- [S] Practical rule: have 2–3 closes per obstacle type that you like saying in your own words.

### 37. Lost? Zoom out — best case / worst case (01:47:23–01:48:45)
- [S] If you're lost in the weeds: "whoa, we've gone to Pluto and back — big picture / let's zoom out." All-purpose close: best case / worst case — "best case you get [outcome]; worst case six weeks of free service and your money back if you hate us."
- [S] Condition: especially strong with any guarantee — "two options, both risk-free: do nothing and you're guaranteed to stay where you are, or take a shot, also risk-free, with a chance of getting what you want." Guarantees "boil the decision down so low it's a simple yes/no".
- [I] Ties to Offers 2021 guarantee chapters (risk reversal as decision simplifier); here framed as a closing tool.

### 38. R — Reinforce the decision: handshake, not handoff; BAMFAM (01:48:45–01:51:03)
- [S] R happens post-swipe/post-purchase: a "handshake" with customer success instead of a "handoff" ("good luck", CS tries to catch it). Information is passed like a baton through the whole lifecycle.
- [S] BAMFAM = book a meeting from a meeting: after every interaction the customer knows exactly when the next meeting is and with whom, and that person knows the whole conversation so far — goals, aspirations, fears, pains — and speaks in those terms.
- [S] Script: "John, this is Sarah, she'll help you with XYZ so you can [goal]; she'll walk you through next steps; she's cured cancer six times…" → Sarah opens with proof-promise-plan personalised to the prospect's pains/aspirations.
- [E] Implemented in a portfolio company (case study made) by switching handoff → handshake. Effects: fewer drop-offs, back-outs, cold feet, first-30-day refunds — "keeping more sales".

### 39. Audience FAQ objections (01:51:03–02:06:35)
Objections were polled from his YouTube audience; he answers each generically (no individual businesses/numbers).
- **Cheap competitor** (01:51:17) — "cheap competitor close": "If ours were the same as theirs, which would you do?" → "yours" → "why?" → they list what makes you better → "right, that's why we're more." Usually gets a laugh; usually a buying question (you're close).
- **The ID close** (01:52:00): in person, ask for their ID before payment. Wallet/purse comes out onto the table → "forgot my wallet/card" becomes impossible; chit-chat over the photo builds rapport; use the ID to get details right (wrong zip on the card loses sales: "is this the same zip as the card you'll use?"); then hold the ID between fingers, gesture at the wallet: "I'll trade you for whichever one you want to use." On Zoom: "can you hold your ID up to the camera so we associate the right profile with the right payment — we try to be PCI compliant" (he admits he made that reason up; "that's how we've always done it" also works).
- **Decision maker** (01:53:44) — spouse/partner/business partner = "I don't have the authority". Prevention: if the purchase materially affects a household/business, involve the partner in the setting process — bring both on the call (B2B or B2C); real estate: you won't close one spouse on a house without the other. [E] Friend selling high-ticket weight loss (to women) gets the partner on the phone and often closes both ("better results together") — a second sale. One-shot four-step process when you must close now: (1) "What would happen if they said no?" (to an egotistical man: "what if they don't give you permission?") — 1 in 3 say "I'd do it anyway" → close; (2) the other 2 in 3 say "then I wouldn't" → "what do you think their main concern would be?" — they voice their own concern through the hypothetical partner (their foil, mirror of yours), then "what would make them say no / an absolute yes / what would it take?" → isolate and close that obstacle; (3) past agreements: "do they know you're here? bought things like this before? do they know you're in pain with this? — if they don't approve of your current situation, why wouldn't they approve of solving it?"; (4) support not permission (final straw): "if the business doesn't grow, who do you blame? if they're in charge they get all the blame, so you're not in control of your own life — not fair to you, them or the relationship." The four arguments: (a) you have authority and don't care, (b) you have authority but were afraid to tell me the real objection, (c) you lack authority but they've already agreed via past behaviour, (d) take authority over your life. Decision-maker is "the hardest of all closes" — the only one where the person says "I can't decide".
- **"Sounds amazing, give me some time / circle back in a couple quarters"** (01:59:31): embedded false truth — it's actually not good enough. "So what are you going to be doing instead?" → "do you think doing that gets you better returns than this?" (framed via foil: "a question I asked someone earlier today"). Theory: time = priorities; you must elevate the value of yours or devalue theirs. If they say yes, ask why; if no, "then you should prioritise this." Ethical rule: the moment a prospect is better served not buying, stop — "this is why you want to sell stuff that works better."
  - [S] "Put math to it": "if we improve conversion 30%, revenue +30%; at 90% gross margin and 30% net margin that doubles profit. Are the things you're working on doubling profit? Over what period — two quarters? If we doubled profit would that let you do those faster?" Seek-to-understand frame, not combative. "Whenever someone gives an answer you can always ask why" → chunk up → "my thing solves that reason better."
  - [S] Distinguish: "give me some time to decide" = decision avoidance (stall); "I'm busy / could do this later" = priorities.
- **No guarantee** (02:02:58): he doesn't guarantee most of what he sells. "Anti-guarantee": "if you're the type of person who needs a guarantee this is definitely not for you — we can't control what you do; is it fair to guarantee work I have no input in?" Principle: the more control you have over the outcome, the more you can guarantee; the less control, the less. Takeaway sale: "this is for people serious about doing it — are you? I can't eat the food or work out for you; hundreds have succeeded; does that sound reasonable/fair?"
  - [I] Tension with Offers 2021, which pushes strong guarantees as a core value-equation lever; here he states he mostly does not offer them and sells around it. Keep both positions.
- **"Let me do your free stuff / lead magnet / the advice from this call first"** (02:04:08): agree — "absolutely, do that before we start / before we kick you off — one of two things happens: you succeed and hit the next problem I can help with, or you don't and I can help with this one; either way, let's get started." Never disagree; move from "I want to do this thing" to "do it inside the program".
- **Enterprise / government exception** (02:05:13): if you sell to government or large enterprises with 6–12-month cycles and multiple stakeholders, "much of the stuff I covered here is not going to apply" — these techniques force a decision now; committees can't decide now. Instead stay in the pocket and multi-thread: the number of people the customer talks to in your org raises close likelihood and smooths transition; one-to-one bond is easily broken, 3-on-their-side × 2-on-yours creates many ties (diagram not recoverable).

### 40. Mindset 1 — the person who cares most about the prospect wins (02:06:35–02:08:28)
- [S] He dislikes "mindset"; reframes each as a behaviour. If they care more about themselves than you care about them, they win. Caring → seek to understand → rapport, tone and conviction follow. "Keep the human first": if it's no longer good for them, tell them not to buy. Learning to turn away prospects raises overall close rate because you know you're helping; trying to close everybody wears on you — "either you become a sociopath or your sales go down". Qualify, never lie.
- [S] Behaviour on "I've got to think about it": "Sarah, I'm trying to figure out how to make this work for you because I genuinely believe it will help — what are you most afraid of having happen?"

### 41. Mindset 2 — volume negates luck (02:08:28–02:11:33)
- [S] Sales is a numbers game (like dating); know the pipeline; the best salespeople do the most volume. Volume ↔ skill loop. Early on, force-feed volume to get through the terrible period fast; mastery gives more ways to reward yourself (a master closer enjoys pulling a sale out of the "red zone").
- [S] Prescription: find the best closer on your team and do double their effort ("barely possible? welcome to getting better") — Kobe Bryant's three-workouts-a-day logic.
- [S] Skill vs income curve (drawn): skill rises through stages of roughly 2–3 months, then ~1 year, then 2–3 years; income lags and then spikes — "all the best returns come at the very end of mastery"; people quit before the gains. If you hate sales you're just not skilled enough yet; the best editors/writers/salespeople all sucked at first. [E] "I didn't close my first sale."

### 42. Mindset 3 — sales résumés don't matter; group interviews; hire for the lowest skill deficiency (02:11:33–02:15:00)
- [E] Twice his sales teams didn't grow fast enough for inbound demand; both times the cause was heavy résumé screening before interviews. He wrote about this in the employees chapter of his Leads book (author's own cross-reference).
- [S] Condition: transactional selling (setting, one-to-two-call closes, short cycles) → look for skills observable in a call, not past experience. Practice: group interviews — invite everyone onto one call, 1–2-minute interviews each; stars speak up, communicate clearly, and the best encourage nervous others (team players). "One of the easiest ways to scale a sales team we've found."
- [S] Attitude vs aptitude: "hire for attitude not aptitude" is only sort of true. Universal rule: hire for the lowest skill deficiency (fastest ramp / highest ROI on training). "Kind", "hardworking", "charismatic" are bundled terms hiding ~20 sub-skills each; teaching kindness takes far longer than the 2–3 weeks needed to teach calling leads and saying a script. Flip condition: for very niche, hard-to-find expertise (level-10 coder, a transactional CFO with 50 deals in your industry) it may be easier to teach culture than the skill. More true for low-level roles, less true higher up.

### 43. Mindset 4 — compensation = recruiting, not performance (02:15:00–02:16:58)
- [S] Comp attracts better closers (smaller skill deficiencies) but doesn't retain them or change behaviour. In his experience no comp change ever changed team behaviour except one: paying on cash collected produces more cash collected. Other schemes fail because the reward cycle is too delayed; behaviour changes come from faster, lower-intensity feedback (phone-addiction analogy) — training and fast feedback loops beat comp redesign.
- [S] Side note: sales announced in Slack are over-represented vs sales actually processed because reps are rewarded for posting "closed!". Pro tip: Zap from CRM "processed sale" → Slack so kudos only fire on real sales.

### 44. Mindset 5 — sales and marketing are one continuum (02:16:58–02:19:35)
- [E] He runs meetings as "revenue generation" under one roof, e.g. a Chief Revenue Officer over acquisition; since doing so for years, "basically no conflict" between sales and marketing.
- [S] Model: a fixed amount of information is required for someone to buy. With perfect knowledge people buy alone (Carvana, Tesla). Whenever marketing suffices for some to self-buy, there's typically an even larger pool who need more selling to close the gap. Sales = personalised marketing: if six things must be known and marketing covers 1–3, the rep covers 4–6; for the next prospect who has 1, 3, 5, the rep covers 2, 4, 6.
- [S] No mysticism about "above $X you must sell by phone" — behaviours cluster by price but nothing is a necessity; still, since more people don't know enough than do, a sales team "will almost always improve overall ROI/ROAS" of any acquisition process.

### 45. Mindset 6 — belief and trust are continuums; bigger plane, longer runway (02:19:35–02:21:40)
- [S] Trust and belief aren't binary ("they didn't trust you") but continuums ("didn't trust you enough"); checklist which actions raise belief, raise trust, or lower trust.
- [S] Ism: trust/belief required for a big purchase is larger than for a small one — a bigger plane needs a longer runway. Diagnostic: if you're getting many 2–4-call closes, you're not educating enough before the first call; fix = more education material / pre-marketing / warming to move them along the continuum so the rep gets "the 6-inch putt".
- [I] Consistent with Leads 2023's warm-up logic (more content → warmer leads → shorter sales cycle); the "plane" metaphor is the transcript's.

### 46. Mindset 7 — say less (02:21:40–02:22:57)
- [S] Prospect should have roughly two-thirds of talk time (Gong and other tools publish ideal ratios; most closers talk less than the prospect — they answer your questions, you don't answer theirs). Let silence sit (cites Steven Bartlett and Chris Williamson as master interviewers); people expound on themselves if you don't respond → more rapport "without you ever saying anything".

### 47. Mindset 8 — "don't be cute" / fundamentals (02:22:57–02:24:53)
- [E] Advice from a Long Island serial private-equity CEO he interviewed during his PE transaction: CEOs "get cute" with trick plays and fumble; "two fat guys in the middle, run to the right" always works.
- [S] Great vs good salespeople: the great never skip the basics — say the script, follow up quickly, keep rapport, ask good questions, listen, let the prospect close themselves, prepare every call, do warm handoffs, refer to notes rather than asking the customer to recap, set the agenda with proof-promise-plan. "Doing the fundamentals is doing a lot of things over and over and never forgetting any of the steps."

### 48. Mindset 9 — no for now ≠ no forever (02:24:53–02:26:15)
- [S] Some objections are legitimate (a higher-return opportunity, a merger, a team spread thin). Dating analogy. Behaviour: pulse-check every 3–6 months without shame — memes / one-line emails; most reps don't follow up because it's work and they fear rejection; if you believe you'll help a qualified prospect, circle back when timing is better.

### 49. Mindset 10 — be a trash man; yellow = gold (02:26:15–02:28:58)
- [S] Lead scoring (used in his companies): approximate probability of closing, often as score and/or colour — green / yellow (slightly qualified, lower quality) / red (unqualified). Example: 800 credit score + $500k income scores higher for a timeshare than someone with six jobs and 19 kids.
- [S] For new reps: take as many calls as the company allows, including yellows — free training on the hardest leads. Reframe "yellows as golds". [E] In-person gym days he sold to homeless people and people 30 days out of a 10-year prison stint; reframed as "advanced-level bosses" — "if I can close someone with no money and no job I've gotten really good."
- [S] Condition: if someone literally cannot benefit, don't sell; if merely less qualified (fewer financial resources), practise closing. "Repetition is the mother of skill"; hard prospects make easy ones easier.

### 50. Mindset 11 — objections do not mean no (02:28:58–02:30:05)
- [S] Especially on price: "wow, that's expensive" is often just a voiced reaction, not an objection — respond "yeah, totally" and continue. People have said "that's expensive" and bought many times. Over-eager reps start overcoming and "waste bullets on zombies that aren't there". Rule: unless they say they don't want to buy, don't assume they don't.

### 51. Mindset 12 — "we don't negotiate with terrorists": never change price to close (02:30:05–02:31:53)
- [S] Never change price to close a deal: customers talk; once they see price move for them they assume every price is negotiable and tell everyone. Price testing is fine but must be premeditated ("next time we'll try X"), never mid-sale.
- [S] Favourite overcome for "can you do it for less?": "we could do it for more" — "never not worked for me… perfect batting average". Asking for a discount is a high purchase-intent signal; the counter re-anchors above the price (they ask −20%, you say +20%), creating fear of missing out — "no no, I'm fine, I'll buy".
- [S] What you can change: terms — a payment plan changes what they pay today; a feature downsell changes the components. Both differ from discounts for no reason.
- [I] Consistent with Money Models 2025 / Offers 2021 downsell logic (remove components rather than cut price); the "we could do it for more" line is this transcript's.

### 52. Mindset 13 — people want to buy (02:31:53–02:32:27)
- [S] People like buying; they hold back only because they were punished for buying in the past. The job is to help them justify the decision — "people want an excuse to buy, you need to give it to them."

### 53. Mindset 14 — closers ask hard questions because they care; kind not nice (02:32:27–02:33:58)
- [S] Kind ≠ nice: nice people avoid offending; kind people want to help, which means changing behaviour and sometimes calling BS. Tactful way: (1) confront hard questions before asking for money, (2) through foils of people like them. Human-first keeps rapport; otherwise "commission breath" — sounding like you prioritise the close over the person — makes them feel incentives are misaligned and you lose them.
