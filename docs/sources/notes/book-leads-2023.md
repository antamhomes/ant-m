## SECTION II: GET UNDERSTANDING

### Section II intro (file 010) — three things: what a lead is; separate money-making leads from time-wasting leads; best ways to get money-making leads to show interest.

### Leads Alone Aren't Enough (file 011, "a lead is a person you can contact")
- [E] The book started because someone asked what a lead was; six months of trying to define it.
- [S] **Lead** = "a person you can contact. That's all." Bought email lists, database contacts, phone contacts, people on the street — all leads.
- [S] **Engaged lead** = a person who *shows* interest in the stuff you sell. Examples: gives contact info on a website; follows you on social media *and* you can contact them; replies to an email campaign.
- [S] "Engaged leads are the true output of advertising." The book is really about engaged leads (title kept simple).
- [S] Words shape thinking → doing; wrong definitions lead to doing the wrong stuff.

### Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets (file 012, "salty pretzels")
- [E] April 2016 webinar attempt: paid $25,000 for a group; mentor: "Do a webinar every week until you make a million dollars." Eight Sundays of building; $150/day on ads; "Three days, $450, 80 leads, and 0 sales later… I shut it all down." A 13-minute screen-recorded case-study video (ad account walk-through, ending "we'll do the whole thing for free… only get paid off the sales you make… book a call") replaced the webinar under the headline "FREE Case Study: How we added 213 members and $112,000 in revenue to a small gym in San Diego." Next morning Leila's calendar was booked solid a week out. Lesson: "you have to give people something they want."
- [S] **Offer** = what you promise to give in exchange for something of value. **Core offer** = product/service for money. "If you advertise your core offer… the direct path to money. Advertising your core offer might be all you need… **Try this way first.**"
- [S] **When to use a lead magnet:** "Sometimes… people want to know more about your offer before they buy. This is common for businesses that sell more expensive stuff." Then advertise with a lead magnet first.
- [S] **Lead magnet** = "a complete solution to a narrow problem." Typically lower-cost or free; once solved it reveals another problem solved by the core offer. Leads who take free/low-cost offers now are more likely to buy a related higher-cost offer later. Pretzel analogy: pretzels solve hunger, reveal thirst; drink revenue covers pretzel cost.
- [S] Quality bar: "valuable enough on its own that you could charge for it," and after getting it they should want more. "A person who pays with their time now is more likely to pay with their money later."
- [S] Claim: good lead magnets get more engaged leads and customers than a core offer alone, for less money.
- [S] "Grand Slam Offers work for free stuff as much or better than they do for paid stuff." You may end up with several insanely valuable offers, some free. "The business that provides the most value wins."

**Framework: Seven Steps To Creating an Effective Lead Magnet** (compact spec)
1. **Figure out the problem you want to solve and who to solve it for.** [S] Tool: the *Problem-Solution cycle* — every problem has a solution; every solution reveals more problems; smaller cycles sit inside larger ones. Rule: pick a problem that is *narrow and meaningful*; solve it; the new problem it reveals must be solvable by your core offer ("if we can solve that new problem with our core offer, we've got a winner"). [E] Own example: this book is a lead magnet; the lead = businesses under $1M annual profit; with enough engaged leads they exceed $1M profit and qualify for the core offer (investment). Example: home-selling — narrow pre-sale problems (what is it worth, increase value, photos, cleaning, landscaping, fixes, moving, staging) are lead-magnet material; solving one for free earns trust; core offer = selling the home.
2. **Figure out how to solve it — three types of lead magnets.** [S]
   - *Reveal their problem* ("think diagnosis"). Works great when it reveals problems that get worse the longer you wait. Examples: website speed test (30% below standard → money lost); posture analysis; termite inspection (sell removal if yes, prevention if no — "win-win").
   - *Samples and trials* — full but brief access to the core offer; limit uses, time, or both. Works great when the core offer is a recurring solution to a recurring problem. Examples: faster server trial; free posture adjustment; consumables (food, cosmetics, medicine — "It's how Costco sells more food").
   - *One step of a multi-step process* — give one valuable step free, the rest when they buy. Works great when the core offer solves a more complex problem. Examples: this book; one of three coats of garage-door sealant; free finance courses/calculators/templates that reveal the time and effort of DIY → sell financial services.
3. **Figure out how to deliver it — four delivery methods.** [S] Software (spreadsheet, calculator, small tool; [E] gym-stats dashboard with industry comparison and rank), Information (courses, lessons, interviews, keynotes, live events, mistakes/pitfalls, hacks; [E] mini course on writing a gym ad), Services (do work free — adjust a back, website audit, first coat of sealant, turn a video into an ebook; [E] run gym owners' ads free for 30 days), Physical products (posture chart, supplement, small sealant bottle, boxing gloves; [E] the book *Gym Launch Secrets*). 3 types × 4 deliveries = "up to twelve lead magnets that solve a single narrow problem." [S] Practice: make as many versions as possible and rotate them — keeps advertising fresh, low effort, shows which works; results often surprising.
4. **Test what to name it.** [S] Ogilvy: headline = 80 cents of the advertising dollar; five times more people read the headline. Improving headline/name/display "can 2x, 3x, or 10x your engagement." Test in this order: headline, image(s), subheadline. [E] Title tests: Round I Advertising ✔ vs Promotion; Round II Advertising vs Leads ✔; Round III Marketing vs Leads ✔. Image: Real ✔ vs Cartoon. Subheadline: "How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff" beat "How to get more people to want to buy your stuff," "How to get more strangers…," "How to get as many leads as you darn well please," and "Get strangers to want to buy your stuff" (the last differs only by "how to"; removing one word "more" also mattered). Reason for "want to buy" not "buy": that's sales, a future book ("$100M Sales" or "Persuasion"). [S] Method if you have a following: polls; few votes give direction. Else post "respond 1 or 2"; else message people directly. Bonus: if people ask when they can get it → "mega winner."
5. **Make it easy to consume.** [S] Ease raises take rates and consumption "2x, 3x, and even 4x+." Software: phone + computer + multiple formats. Information: every format (images, video, text, audio). Services: more times of day, more days, video/phone/in person. Physical: simple ordering, fast shipping, easy to open, simple directions (Apple). [E] *$100M Offers* splits nearly ¼/¼/¼/¼ across ebook, physical, audiobook, video (free on Acquisition.com) — "the easiest way I know to get 2-3-4x the amount of leads for the same work."
6. **Make it darn good — "Give Away The Secrets, Sell The Implementation."** [S] "You can never provide too much value. But you can provide too little." Goal: provide more value than the cost of your core offer *before* they've bought. Fear of giving away secrets → you give "sucky fluff" → prospects think you suck and tell others. People buy based on expected value after buying; easiest way to raise that expectation = value before they buy. [E] A company that scaled $1M→$10M on free content is very likely to partner with Acquisition.com. [S] "99% of people aren't gonna buy, but they will create (or destroy) your reputation based on the value of your free stuff." Make lead magnets as good as paid stuff.
7. **Make it easy for them to tell you they want more — the CTA.** [S] Good CTAs have (1) what to do and (2) reasons to do it right now. What to do: clear, simple, direct ("call now," not "don't delay"). Reasons: good reasons beat bad ones, but any reason beats none; stack as many effective reasons as possible.
   - *Scarcity* — limited supply vs demand raises desire; but fewer units = fewer engaged leads before you run out. Best strategy: **reality / ethical scarcity** — "If you sold 1000x the customers tomorrow, could you handle it?" Advertise the natural limits (customer service, onboarding, inventory, time slots). Examples: "The most convenient class times fill up fast. Call now…"; "I can only handle five people per week…"; one-batch shirts never reprinted.
   - *Urgency* — short time to act; deadlines; discounts/bonuses that expire after X minutes/hours, "never available again." Examples: July 4th promo ends Monday midnight; Black Friday four hours left; free hat through Friday for 3+ books.
   - *Fraternity Party Planner — make up a reason* (author's favourite). Any reason works better than none; [E] cites a Harvard line-cutting experiment: more people let someone cut with any reason; more still with a sensible reason. "Think 'the stuff you say' after the word because" — "Because moms know best," "Because it's my birthday."
   - Rule: "Don't be clever, be clear." Give the CTA often.
- [S] **Lead-magnet money math** (worked example): core offer earns $10,000 profit; $1,000 in ads to get someone on a call; close 1 in 3 → $3,000 to get a customer. Alternative: free lead magnet costing $25 to deliver; more engage, so only $75 of ads per call → $100 per call all-in ("ten times more engaged leads for the same cost… this happens all the time when you nail the lead magnet"). If 1 in 10 lead-magnet takers buys → CAC $1,000 ($100 × 10) → "cut our cost to get a customer by 3x"; $10,000 profit / $1,000 = 10:1 return; same ad budget → "triple our business." "Even if your lead magnet costs money to deliver, it should still lower your cost to get a new customer." Experienced owners win because a $25 delivery budget lets you provide far more value than $0.
- [S] Action-step recap adds **Step 0: "If you're struggling to get leads, make an amazing lead magnet."** Step 7 restated: "Tell them what to do next, why it's a good idea, do it clearly, and do it often."
- [S] **Section II conclusion — a good lead magnet does four things:** (1) engages ideal customers when they see it; (2) gets more people to engage than the core offer alone; (3) is valuable enough that they consume it; (4) makes the right people more likely to buy.
- [I] Cross-source: the "value equation" and "Grand Slam Offer" are imported from Offers 2021 without re-derivation; this chapter's "lead magnet as complete solution to a narrow problem" is the same logic applied to a free front-end.

## SECTION III: GET LEADS — THE CORE FOUR

### Section III intro (file 013, "the only four ways")
- [S] Two audience types: **Warm** = people who gave you permission to contact them (friends, family, followers, current/previous customers, contacts). **Cold** = people who have not given permission ("strangers" — other people's audiences: buying lists, making lists, paying platforms for access).
- [S] Two communication paths: **1-to-1 (private)** — phone call, email; **1-to-many (public)** — posts, billboards, podcasts. Automation does not change the nature: emailing a 10,000 list "once" is "one-to-one really fast by a machine."
- [S] **The Core Four** (the only four ways to let anyone know about anything): 1-to-1 warm = Warm Outreach; 1-to-many warm = Posting Content; 1-to-1 cold = Cold Outreach; 1-to-many cold = Paid Ads.
- [S] Diagnostic rule: "if you aren't getting as many leads as you want, you're not doing the core four with enough skill or with enough volume."

### #1 Warm Outreach (file 014, "Reach. Out. To. One. Hundred. People. Every. Day.")
- [E] May 2013: $51,128.13 in savings; skipped Harvard MBA ($150,000 loans, two years no income). Set up "Impetus Group LLC" (renamed "The Free Training Project"). First offer by call/text/Facebook message: "do you know anyone who's trying to get into shape? I'm training people for free for twelve weeks… custom nutrition plan and grocery list. All they have to do is donate to a charity of their choice and let me use their testimonial." Six yeses (two high-school friends, one college friend, three they referred). After 12 weeks asked them to pay him instead — nobody minded. Their referrals brought another 5–6 clients, paying directly. Business made ~$4,000/month, replaced his job income.
- [S] Starting requirements: "a tax ID, a bank account, a way to take payments, and a way to communicate with people" — the last is how you get leads.
- [S] **Definition:** one-to-one contact with your warm audience. "The cheapest and easiest way to find people interested in the stuff you sell… most businesses don't do it." Forms: calls, texts, emails, DMs, voicemails. You advertise either your lead magnet or your core offer.
- [S] Character: few engaged leads per unit time (personal, manual) but *reliable* — "As certain as the sun rises and sets, it works." Works with 100 contacts or 1,000,000; later scaled with automation and employees (Section IV).
- [S] **When:** "a fantastic way to get your 'First Five Clients' for any new product or service. Advanced folks: think re-engagement and new product lines."

**Framework: How To Do Warm Reach Outs in 10 Steps**
1. *Get your list* — everyone has one: phone contacts, every email account's contacts, every social platform's followers/friends/connections. Add them up; "for many of you, this will be your first 1000 leads."
2. *Pick a platform* — the one with the most contacts; you will hit all eventually.
3. *Personalize your greeting* — use something you know about them as the actual reason to reach out; check their profiles if needed. "You haven't asked for anything. You're just checking in and providing value." Ex: "Saw you just had a baby! Congrats!…"
4. *Reach out to 100 people per day* with personalized messages (call, text, email, DM, postcard). Follow up **up to three times: once per day for three days, or until they respond**, whichever first. Physical mail: once per week.
5. *Warm them up — act like a human, the A-C-A framework:* **Acknowledge** (restate what they said: "Two kids. And you're an accountant…"), **Compliment** (tie to a positive trait: "Supermom! So hardworking!…"), **Ask** another question that leads toward your offer (therapy: "Do you get time for yourself?"; fitness: "Do you have time to get workouts in?"; cleaning: "Do you have anyone who helps you keep the house tidy?"). People love talking about themselves and being complimented → like and trust you more.
6. *Make them an offer / invite their friends* — after a "normal" amount of conversation: **3–4 exchanges** by phone/message or **3–4 minutes** in person. Offer built on the **value equation** (imported from Offers): Dream Outcome; Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (results, reviews, awards, endorsements, certifications, guarantees "are huge"); Time Delay; Effort and Sacrifice. Maximize the first two, minimize the second two: "You have exactly what they want / They're guaranteed to get it / Insanely fast / Without lifting a finger or giving up anything they love" — get as close as possible without lying.
   - **Script (long form):** "…By the way, do you know anybody who is (describe their struggles) looking to (dream outcome) in (time delay)? I'm taking on five case studies for free, because that's all I can handle. I just want to get some testimonials for my service/product. I help them (dream outcome) without (effort and sacrifice). It works. I even guarantee people get (dream outcome) or I work with them until they do. I just had a girl named XXX work with me (dream outcome) even though she (same struggle). I also had another guy who (dream outcome) and it was his first time. I'd just like more testimonials to show it works across different scenarios. Does anyone you like come to mind? (Pause)… and if no: Haha, well… does anyone you hate come to mind?"
   - Key feature: **you don't ask them to buy — you ask if they know anyone.** "Of the people who say yes, most say they are interested." Engineered to raise perceived likelihood of achievement via results from people like them; they connect the dots; no pushiness. Three outcomes (they're interested / they refer / both) — all wins.
   - **Script (short form):** "I help (ideal customer) get (dream outcome) in (time period) without (effort and sacrifice) and (increase perceived likelihood of achievement)." Works for email, text, DM, call, in person.
7. *Make them the easiest offer in the world — free.* "Don't try to look advanced if you're not." Script: "Since I'm only taking on five people, I can give you all the attention you need to get brag-worthy results. And I'll give it all for free so long as you promise to: 1) Use it 2) Give me feedback and 3) Leave a killer review if you think it deserves one. Does that sound fair?"
   - [S] Rule: **whenever you launch a new product or service, make the first five free** (exact number matters less than the why): you get reps making offers; "you probably suck (for now)" and people forgive free; guinea pigs to work out kinks. Free customers who get value are more likely to leave reviews/testimonials, give feedback, send friends and family; they make you money three ways: convert to paying, send paying referrals, testimonials bring paying customers.
   - *If they say no:* the most expensive part is usually the **hidden costs** (time, effort, sacrifice — bottom of the value equation). If you can't give it away, either they don't want it (dream outcome), don't believe you (PLA), or hidden costs are too high — "your 'free' stuff is too expensive." Ask "why?" and "What would I have to do to make it worth it for you to continue?" Failure is required; "rack up failures as fast as you can… paying down your 'no tax.'" "Yeses give me opportunity. Nos give me feedback."
8. *Start back at the top* — after one platform, go to the platform with the second-most contacts, then third, etc. [S] Math: 1,000 leads = "ten solid days of work. A month of work including follow-ups." By then "five or more people will have accepted your free offer," some converting to paid; if you did a good job they send friends who also pay.
9. *Start charging* — **litmus test: "Once people start referring, start charging."** Swap "free" for "80% off for the next five," then "60% off for the next five," then "40% off…" and so on — the **"I increase my prices every five" rule** adds real urgency. You may keep raising 20% every five until you find your sweet spot.
10. *Keep your list warm* — give regular value (email, social) so the list stays primed. Then probe with Dean Jackson's **"9-word email"**: "Are you still looking to [4 word desire]?" — no images, no frills, no links, "just a question." [E] "Among the first things I do when I invest in a new business." Examples: "…buy your dream home? / get more sales leads? / tone up your arms? / open an online store? / start a YouTube channel?" Replies = engaged leads = **top priority for warm reach outs**. "If you treat them well, your audience will feed you forever."

- [S] **Benchmarks (warm outreach):** ~1 in 5 contacts engage (100 reach outs → ~20 replies); ~1 in 5 of repliers take the free offer (→ 4); of 4 free takers convert ~1 to a paid offer later → **~1 customer per 100 warm reach outs**. Numbers vary with offer value and trust; volume guarantees a customer; numbers improve with practice.
- [S] **Money math ("$100,000+ per year with nothing else"):** assume 1% of list buys a $400 offer. 500 reach outs/week → 5 customers/week → $2,000/week → $104,000/year ("two times the median household income in the US").
- [S] "You'll learn more in the first ten days of doing 100 reach outs than… everything you've ever read."
- [S] **Time rule when starting out:** getting new customers should take the majority of your time — "four hours per day, minimum… the first thing you do when you get up."
- [S] Two limitations of warm outreach: time; the number of people who know you (you'll "run out") → next: posting free content.

### #2 Post Free Content Part I — How To Build An Audience (file 015, "the audience is the compounding asset")
- [E] 2020: audience-built fortunes (Kylie Jenner, Huda Kattan $600M valuation, Proper 12 $600M in 12 months, Teremana $3.5B) vs the Hormozis' $13M personal income the prior year; a famous friend's "I'd pay that price any day" line pushed them all in on personal brands.
- [S] Core belief to transfer: "If someone is making more money than you, they are better at the game of business in some way. Take it as good news."
- [S] "The content you create isn't the compounding asset — the audience is." Content disappears; audience keeps growing.
- [E] Bought knowledge: Leila paid $120,000 for four calls with a big influencer. Call 1: post regularly on every platform → +200,000 audience in 12 months. Call 2: "anyone telling you there's some secret is trying to sell you something. We just put out as much as we possibly can" (he posted 1x/day on Instagram vs the influencer's 3x; 1x/week on LinkedIn vs 5x/day). Next six months: 10x the content → +1.2M audience; "my audience grew ten times as fast."
- [S] **How it works:** warm outreach reaches ten people by repeating ten times; free content says it once and reaches all ten. Those who find it valuable join your warm audience; those who think others will value it share it; infinite sharing. Occasionally make an offer; bigger audience = more money.
- [S] Three consequences: posting grows your warm audience; constant posting = constantly growing audience of likely buyers; **free content makes all other advertising more effective** (a prospect who finds lots of valuable content is more likely to buy; one who finds none, less).
- [S] Trade-offs: harder to personalize → fewer respond; you compete with everyone posting; if you stand out you get copied → constant innovation.

**Framework: The Content Unit — Hook, Retain, Reward**
- [S] All audience-growing content rewards the consumer; a person is rewarded only if they (1) have a reason to consume, (2) pay attention long enough, (3) get the reason satisfied → you must (a) hook, (b) retain, (c) reward. **Content unit** = the smallest amount of material that hooks, retains and rewards (can be one image, meme, sentence, jingle — all three at once).
- **1) Hook** — measured by the % of people who start consuming. Three levers: topics, headlines, format.
  - *Topics* (prefer personal experience: "there's only one of you"): **Far Past** (important life lessons tied to your product; "give them the story without the scar"; [E] the "I found you some time" phone-usage story: hook = complaining about no time while glued to phone; retain = friend shows 3 hours/day on social; reward = "Hey, I found you some time"); **Recent Past** (do stuff, then talk about it — mine your calendar, meetings, warm-outreach conversations; take notes/recordings; testimonials and case studies belong here); **Present** (write ideas down the moment they come; author tweets ideas publicly and makes more on what over-performs); **Trending** (go where attention is; add your experience/expertise; effective for broader audiences); **Manufactured** (pick an interesting topic, learn/make/do it, show it; costliest, biggest payouts; e.g. "I lived on $100 for a month").
  - *Headlines* — from a meta-analysis of news, include at least two of: **Recency** (an hour ago beats a year ago), **Relevancy** (personally meaningful — nurses vs accountants), **Celebrity** (Bezos's breakfast), **Proximity** (neighbour's house on fire), **Conflict** (pineapple on pizza; hero vs villain; freedom vs security), **Unusual** (six-fingered man), **Ongoing** (labour updates every ten minutes).
  - *Format* — "make your content look like what they expect will reward them"; match the platform's best content first, then tweak for your ideal audience; use the top content in your market on that platform as the guide. (Illustrating meme not in text.)
- **2) Retain** — favourite driver: **curiosity** (people will wait years; daily messages about the sales book). Embed unresolved questions (explicit or implicit) via **Lists** ("Top 10 Mistakes", "7 Ways I invested $1000 in my 20s…", "28 Ways To Stay Poor" — give the count in the headline/first seconds), **Steps** (actions in order to a goal — "3 Steps to Creating a Great Hook"; steps are less flexible, more explicit reward; lists more flexible, less explicit reward), **Stories** (real or imaginary; past/might/never happen; "My editor made me do 19 drafts…"). Interweave freely (lists within steps, story per item).
- **3) Reward** — "How good your content is depends on how often it rewards your audience in the time it takes them to consume it. Think value per second." **"There is no such thing as too long, only too boring."** Bad reward examples: promise 7 ways and give 4, or 7 stale ones, or to the wrong audience (single guys, dentists who can't use them). "The audience decides." Test of success: **your audience grows; if not, your stuff isn't that good.** Completely answer the questions you embedded.
- [S] **Short vs long form:** "not much" difference — long content = content units linked together (each step of a five-step list can be a unit). Longer = more hook-retain-reward cycles in a row = more skill (new comedian gets minutes; master gets an hour). Advice: start with shorter versions (novelists started with short stories).

### #2 Post Free Content Part II — Monetize Your Audience (file 016, "Give until they ask")
- [S] **Give : Ask ratio.** Gary Vaynerchuk's "jab, jab, jab, right hook": deposit goodwill with rewarding content, withdraw with offers. Benchmarks: television ≈ 13 minutes ads per 60 → 47:13 ≈ **3.5:1**; Facebook ≈ **4 posts per 1 ad**. These are *mature* platforms optimizing money over growth → treat as the **minimum sustainable ratio** ("give, give, give, ask" ≈ maximal monetization without shrinking). Growing platforms "dramatically over give and under ask." **If you want to grow, give far more than you ask.**
- [S] Author's tweak: **"Give until they ask."** Not asking builds trust and sharing; discomfort of receiving without giving back makes people come to you (website, DM, email). Benefits: best customers (biggest givers), growth never slows. "You give in public, ask in private." Bottom line: "The moment you start asking for money is the moment you decide to slow down your growth. So the more patient you are, the more you will get when you finally make your ask."
- [S] **How to ask (if you must):** asks are "commercials" — you interrupt your own content; cost = potential trust loss, slower growth, time to build the audience; money-wise free. Two strategies:
  - **Integrated** — an ask inside every piece, keeping the give:ask ratio high. [E] 1-hour podcast with 3 × 30-second ads = 58.5 min giving : 1.5 min asking, "well above the 3:1 ratio." [E] Warning: a friend's fast-growing podcast started asking too frequently *in* the content → stopped growing, then shrank. Placement: after a valuable moment or at the end; add the first, verify audience growth doesn't slow, then add a second, etc.
  - **Intermittent** — many pure "give" pieces, then an occasional "ask" piece (e.g. 10 gives, ask on the 11th).
  - **Platform rule:** short-form platforms → intermittent dominates; long-form platforms → integration is usually best.
- [S] What you ask for: core offer or lead magnet. "Don't overcomplicate this." Lead-magnet example: after a post about getting leads — "I have 11 more tips… Go to my site to grab a pretty visual of them"; the thank-you page after opt-in shows the paid offer with an explainer video; bonus if the lead magnet is relevant to the content advertising it.
  - **Core-offer ask script (modelled on the warm-outreach offer):** "I'm looking for 5 (specific avatar) to help achieve (dream outcome) in (time delay). The best part is, you don't have to (effort and sacrifice). And if you don't get (dream outcome), I will do two things (increase perceived likelihood of achievement): 1) I will hand you your money back 2) I will work with you until you do. I do this because I want everyone to have an amazing experience with us and because I'm confident I can deliver on my promise. If that sounds fair, DM me/book a call/comment below/reply to this email/etc." Then "get back to providing value."
  - Decision rule: pick integrated vs intermittent; pick core offer vs lead magnet — "If you're not sure, do the lead magnet. It's lower risk."
- [S] **How to scale — two opposing, both-valid strategies:**
  - **Depth then width:** (1) post on a relevant platform; (2) post regularly; (3) maximize quality and quantity there — short form "sometimes… up to ten times per day per platform," long form "up to five days per week (see soap operas)"; (4) add another platform while maintaining the first; (5) repeat until all relevant platforms maximized. Advantages: maximize return on one figured-out platform; compounding; fewer resources. Disadvantages: less low-hanging fruit from new platforms/audiences; no "omnipresence"; **single-channel risk** ("platforms change all the time and sometimes ban you for no reason. If you only have one way to get customers, it can kill your business").
  - **Width then depth:** (1)(2) same; (3) move to the next platform while maintaining the previous; (4) until on all relevant platforms; (5) maximize all at once. Advantages: broader reach faster; repurposing (one short video formatted for all short-video platforms). Disadvantages: more labour/attention/time; risk of "lots of bad content everywhere. Sucky fluff."
  - [S] Stage note: "If you already have a sizable business, scale up faster." "The best day to start posting content was the day you were born. The second best day is today."
- [E] **Why make content even if it's not your primary method:** January 2020 — paid ads stopped working; Leila asked "What did we stop doing in the months before the decline?" Answer: Alex stopped making gym content and talked general business. Survey of gym-owner clients: **78% had consumed at least two long-form pieces of content before booking a call.** Free content "was nurturing the demand" and warms people going to and coming from the cold methods. Bottom line: content makes all other advertising return more, even if hard to measure.
- [S] **7 lessons from making content:**
  1. Switch "How to" → "How I"; "the best way" → "my favorite ways" (especially starting out). Experience can't be questioned; avoids preachiness. ("How I Built My 7-Figure Agency" vs "How To Build…")
  2. "We need to be reminded more than we need to be taught." [E] He posts about his book daily; a survey found 1 in 5 who saw the post didn't know he had a book. "You'll get bored of your content before your whole audience even sees it."
  3. **Puddles, Ponds, Lakes, Oceans** — narrow the focus. Stage rule: "If you have a small local business, you probably shouldn't make general business content. Not at first." Narrow to what you do and where (plumbing in a certain town → king of that puddle) → expand to local-business pond → brick-&-mortar-chains lake → general-business ocean.
  4. Content creates tools for salespeople: keep a "greatest hits" master list labelled by the problem it solves and benefit; sales team sends before/after calls, especially pieces resolving common concerns.
  5. Free content retains paying customers: buyers are more likely to consume your free content and include it in their ROI calculation; bad free content makes them like the paid product less.
  6. "People don't have shorter attention spans, they have higher standards" — streaming proves hours of bingeing; make good stuff.
  7. Avoid pre-scheduling posts: manually posted content performs better (immediate feedback pressure makes you try harder); someone should press "submit."
- [S] **Benchmarks (content):** measure monthly (1) total followers and reach (how big), (2) rate of growth (how fast): 500 new / 1,000 start = 50% growth; 5,000 new reach / 10,000 = 50%. "We can only control inputs" → pick a posting cadence per platform and an "ask cadence," then "Do. Not. Stop." Measure both absolute and relative growth ("The more ways you measure, the more ways you can win" — Dr. Kashey).
- [E] Podcast: twice a week for four years before the Top 100 list; in year five *The Game* became Top 10 US business, top 500 world. "Give time, time."
- [S] Your first post can be an ask (you've likely already provided value); if it doesn't produce an engaged lead, give for a while, then ask once you've earned it.
- [S] Sequencing rule: content is less predictable than but complementary to warm outreach; **keep doing warm reach outs** — content grows the warm audience, which feeds more warm reach outs. Commit to both daily. "With these two methods alone [you] can eventually build a six- or seven-figure business."

### #3 Cold Outreach (file 018, "Quantity has a quality all of its own")
- [E] July 2020: COVID closed brick-and-mortar customers (30% of customers went out of business within months); Apple's update crippled ads. A rejected sales hire revealed a gym-software company running "100% cold outreach" with ~30 reps at ~$10,000,000 per month selling a retention (not money-making) offer. Alex gave him runway to build cold outreach; he promised profitability in 12 weeks. Results: September 0 sales; October 2 ($32,000; team asked to pull the plug); December 4 ($64,000; asked again); January 6 ($96,000); February 10 ($160,000); March 14 ($224,000); April 20 ($320,000); May 30 ($480,000); "Today: Cold outreach generates millions per month." Used every legal method: cold calls, emails, DMs, voicemails.
- [S] Five lessons: (1) another company in his space made far more — broke his belief about market size; (2) they grew entirely through *private* advertising, so they operated in secret; (3) if they could, so could he; (4) **expectations: veterans said a year to scale; he thought 12 weeks; it took almost a year** — "Cold outreach takes a long time"; (5) two earlier attempts failed; working with someone who had done it before was decisive.
- [S] **When:** "you'll want one of two things. Either… grow faster than you currently are. Or… increase the predictability of your lead flow." Cold outreach "sits atop the foundation of warm outreach" — the more advanced cousin, no longer limited by your warm audience. Any 1-to-1 channel works: 100 doors, 100 calls, 100 DMs, 100 voicemails. "It worked 100 years ago. It works today."
- [S] **Key difference from warm: trust.** Three new problems: (1) no way to contact them; (2) even if you can, they ignore you; (3) even with attention, they're not interested. Solve in order: get a way to contact → figure out what to say → contact them until they're ready and able to listen. "Cold outreach is a numbers game."

**Problem #1 — Build a list** [S]
- Unlike warm methods, you pick targets (hedge fund managers over $1B; golf apparel retailers over $3M; influencers over 50,000 page views/month).
- Three sources, worked in order of accessibility: **Step 1 Software** — subscribe to as many scraping tools as possible; search by criteria; **test a representative sample (a few hundred) from each**; if contact info is current, leads responsive and as described → pull as many as possible. **Step 2 Brokers** — ask multiple list brokers for lists by criteria; test samples; stick with those that work. **Step 3 Elbow grease** — join groups/communities holding your audience; find contact info in directories/profiles; prefer contacting outside the group ("so I don't come off as someone solely trying to milk the group"), but inside if needed.
- Trade-off: searchable databases are searchable by everyone; **self-assembled lists are the "freshest"** (fewest prior cold reach outs) but cost the most time. Action: search "outbound leads scraping tool"/"database lead scraping"; **put your first 1,000 names together**; "If you have more time than money, you might want to start at step three."

**Problem #2 — What to say: Personalize, then Give Big Fast Value** [S]
- Two factors because strangers don't know or trust you; overcome both "in a matter of seconds."
- **Personalize ("act like you know them")** — make the cold reach out look like a warm one: 1–3 pieces of information a friend might know; compliment them on it; ideally show how it benefited you. Used in email subject lines, first chat messages, first sentences on a call. [E] Dog-trainer call illustration — the only surviving cold-call script in the text: local area code; "<Your name?>" + pause; "it's Alex… I watched a few of your videos and read that recent blog post… That peanut butter trick really helped"; then the company intro, a nearby proof ("John's Doggy Daycare… 100 appointments in 30 days"), "Do you offer similar services?", a light joke ("you wouldn't be upset with me would you?"), and a time-bound close ("walk you through the entire thing soup to nuts later today. Will you be around at 4?"). Opening with "wanna buy some marketing services?" → hang-up. Action: research each lead (yourself, paid help, or software); **batch** the work.
- **Big Fast Value** — strangers give far less time and need more incentive; "We're not trying to tickle their interest, we're trying to blow their minds in under thirty seconds." Offer the core offer, a lead magnet, or both. Named "big fast value" deliberately: mediocre lead magnets blend in and get ignored. [E] First four months of cold outreach "felt like torture" with a "game planning session" (code for sales call) as lead magnet; swapping to "as much free service as we could possibly afford" — **take rates 3x'd** and cold outreach "became a monster channel." Rule: "If your offer/lead magnet isn't working… up the ante." "Give away something for free people would normally pay for" — not "so good they should pay," but "stuff they actually pay for."
- Script notes: phone and chat scripts "never more than a page or two"; cold emails "rarely more than half a page." **Get your first 100 conversations or 10,000 emails out of the way before tweaking.** (Sample scripts announced but absent from extracted text.)

**Problem #3 — Volume** [S]
- Three ways: automate delivery; automate distribution; follow up more times in more ways.
- *Automated delivery* (pre-recorded voice memo/voicemail/video, templated email/text) vs manual (live call, personal voice memo, handwritten letter). Automation = more engaged leads per unit time even if a lower % engage; cold audiences are so large you needn't worry about "burning through."
- *Automated distribution* (robot dialers, 1,000-message blasts) vs manual. "Automate when ethical and available."
- **Personalization vs scale rule:** "you sacrifice personalization for scale… The fewer leads you have, the less automation you should use." Ex: only 1,000 qualifying hedge fund managers → personalize every one; women 25–45 trying to lose weight (tens of millions) → less personalization acceptable (but personalizing still gets more).
- *Technology rule:* allocate **10–20% of effort to brand-new untested technology** (e.g. calling five days/week → try a new dialer on one day and compare).
- *Follow up — more times, more ways.* Most people don't. "Act like you're actually trying to get ahold of these people." Different people respond to different channels (he never answers calls, replies to DMs). Multiple contacts in multiple ways signal seriousness and importance; curiosity/fear of missing out. **Author's order: email first** — because most don't respond, which gives a reason to follow up by another method ("I'm calling you to follow up about my email"). **Expect 2–3 conversations before a higher-ticket sale** with strangers ("Shoot for less, but expect more when you start out").
- *Restart the list:* after contacting everyone multiple times multiple ways, **wait three to six months, then do it again.** Three reasons: they may not have seen it; bad moment; circumstances changed (skinny-jeans → ten pounds after the holidays). "Everything may be right except the timing."
- [S] Author's intended sequence: warm reach outs → reps → content → more reps → then cold reach outs.

**Benchmarks — cold outreach** [S]
- The two failed attempts were run by people who didn't track metrics; the third tracked everything. "The person who runs it… has to know the metrics of the sales process like the back of their hand. Every single stat."
- *Phone example:* 100 cold calls/day; 20% pick-up; 25% of those take the lead magnet → "four engaged leads" (author's figure; [I] 100 × 20% × 25% = 5 — the book's text says four); if 4 hours of calling → 1 engaged lead per hour. Hand-off rule: once the engaged leads that convert make more than the cost of a cold-outreach rep, teach someone else (Section IV). **Threshold: "at least three times the lifetime profit of a customer compared to what it costs you to get them."**
- *Email example:* 100 personalized emails/day; 30% open; 10% of openers reply with interest → 3 engaged leads (30% × 10% = 3%). **"Shoot for 3% of your list turning into engaged leads."** [E] Portfolio campaign for "a very niche high ticket service business": 4% engagement; presumably a third convert → **1 new customer per 100 outreach attempts.**
- *Direct message example:* personal video or voice memo to 100 people (say their name + one personal line, then standard message); 20% reply → 20 engaged leads; qualify with A-C-A. Same 3x rule = "bare minimum"; [E] that portfolio company gets "over 30:1 returns from its outreach efforts."
- *Costs (labour-intensive; nearly all labour + software):* worked example — reps paid $15/hour + $50 per shown appointment; $3,600 profit per sale; leads cost $0.10; a rep calls 200 leads/day → ~2 shows/day; 8-hour day → $120 labour + $100 show commissions + $20 leads = $240 for 2 shows = **$120 per show**; close 33% of shows → **$360 per client (excluding sales commissions)**; $3,600 profit → **10:1 return**. "Then, you just add bodies. It is boring and tedious, but brutally effective."
- [S] **Seven benefits of cold outreach:** (1) no need for lots of content/ads — one perfectly crafted message, no ad fatigue/banner blindness; (2) competitors can't see it — you operate in secret; (3) incredibly reliable: "for every X people contacted, you get Y customers. Then, you simply solve for X" — [E] 1 customer per 100 emails; 100 customers → 10,000 emails = 333/day; one person sends 111/day → three people; (4) fewer platform changes (cold calling, door knocking, cold email rules "hardly changed in thirty years"); (5) less painful compliance — only FTC, no platform rules; banned industries (tobacco, firearms, cannabis, weight loss) can still advertise; "If you have a phone, you can make money"; (6) no spokesperson → sellable business ([E] the established outreach team is why Gym Launch could be sold); (7) hard to copy — invisible steps (list scraping, personalization, distribution software, hiring/training a team) compound.
- [S] When to start: "as you run out of people to advertise to, or, because you just want more."

### #4 Run Paid Ads Part I: Making An Ad (file 019, "Advertising is the only casino where… you become the house")
- [E] June 2013: cold-emailed 40+ gym owners to shadow; only Sam replied (minimum wage job). Bet: $1,000 to test Facebook ads, split profit. Ad text (all caps, no image): "I'M LOOKING FOR 5 CHINO HILLS RESIDENTS TO TAKE PLACE IN A FREE 6 WEEK CHALLENGE. YOU MUST LET US USE YOUR BEFORE AND AFTER PICTURES IN OUR MARKETING IN EXCHANGE FOR THE PROGRAM. CLICK LINK TO SIGN UP." Leads within hours; called all, texted an hour before appointments; sold 19 at $299 = just under $5,700 from $1,000; Sam wrote a $2,500 check. The "6 week challenge" became the biggest gym-industry promotion for seven years: at least $1.5B revenue; taught to 4,500+ gyms; 10,000+ more used unlicensed versions.
- [S] **How paid ads work:** 1-to-many to cold audiences; "renting eyeballs or earballs"; you trade money for reach; the fastest way to reach the most people; riskier, but "can get you more leads than any other method." **Reach is guaranteed; getting your money back isn't → a game of efficiency, not reach.** "The question isn't 'do ads work?' it's 'how well can you make them work?'" Colder audiences → smaller % respond → offset with volume. **"If an ad isn't profitable, most of the time, it's because the right people never saw it."**
- [S] Method = narrowing the haystack: pick a platform with your audience → target within it → craft the ad to repel everyone else → tell the rest what to do. Then **expand puddle → pond → lake → ocean**: bigger audiences have more wrong people but more right ones; the spend:buy ratio falls but total profit rises ($1,000 → $10,000 with $9,000 profit vs $100,000 → $300,000 with $200,000 profit). Rule: "make the audience as big as possible while still turning a profit."
- [S] Four problems: where to advertise; getting the right audience to see it; making the best ad; getting permission to contact them.

**Step 1 — Platform: four things must be true** [S]: (1) you've used it and gotten value as a consumer; (2) you can target people interested in your stuff; (3) you know how to format ads for it; (4) you have the minimum spend. Start with one; begin consuming ads there to learn.

**Step 2 — Targeting** [S]
- Platform choice already excludes ~99% of the world (100M-user platform); language excludes more (99.5%). "Specific is good." One goal: get the highest number of likely buyers to see the ad.
- Two in-platform methods, combinable: (1) **Lookalike audience** from an uploaded list (bigger, higher-quality list → more responsive lookalike). **List order to hit the platform minimum: current + previous customers → add warm reach-out list → add cold reach-out leads.** Forcing size may make it broad — acceptable because of (2) **factor targeting** (age, income, gender, interests, time, location): exclude ages that never buy; car parts during car shows/channels; pets interest.
- Trade-off: more filters → more specific → more efficient but faster "burn" through the list; specificity gives early wins that fund broader audiences later — "This is how you scale."
- Action: consolidate all lead lists, separated into customers, warm outreach, cold outreach; later add "engaged with paid ads but didn't buy." If no lookalike possible, target interests.

**Step 3 — What the ad says: Call Out + Value + CTA** [S]
- Author consumes ads on purpose to study three things: how they call out ideal customers, present value elements, give the CTA.
- **1) Call Out.** "People noticing your ad is the most important part of the ad… by a lot." "The purpose of each second of the ad is to sell the next second… the headline is the first sale." [E] "My advertising became 20x more effective when I focused the majority of my effort on the first five seconds" — the part he tests most. Cocktail-party effect: your name cuts through noise. Callouts range from hyper-specific (your name) to universal (dropped tray); aim "specific enough to get the right people and broad enough to get as many of them as I can."
  - *Verbal callouts:* **Labels** (group words: "Clark County Moms," "Gym Owners," "Remote Workers," "I'm looking for XYZ"; **local rule: "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON"** — "Americans < Texans < Dallas Residents < Irving Residents"; "one of my all-time favorite ways"); **Yes-Questions** ("Do you wake up to pee more than once a night?", "Do you have a home worth over $400,000?"); **If-Then statements** ("If you run over $100,000 per month in ads, we can save you 20% or more…"; class-action style); **Ridiculous Results** ("Massage studio books out two years in advance. Clients furious." "This woman lost 50 pounds eating pizza and fired her trainer").
  - *Nonverbal callouts:* **Contrast** (bright shirt > dull; attractive > plain; moving > still); **Likeness** (show people who look/talk/act like the audience; broad base → many ethnicities/ages/genders; narrow → look like them; "Quack like a duck" — to attract plumbers, dress/talk/be in a plumbing environment; doctor ads: lab coat, stethoscope, facility; **mascots** don't age, never ask for raises, never take days off — Mickey Mouse, Geico Gecko, Tony the Tiger, Michelin Man; *Advanced:* using a likeness that isn't you makes the business less dependent on you and more sellable; "pretty people convert better anyways"); **The Scene** (tossing in bed → sleep troubles; pear + hourglass → pear-shaped body; junk to the ceiling; rock hitting a window; local landmark).
- **2) Value — the What-Who-When framework** (requires knowing the value equation "forwards and backwards"). Ogilvy: "The customer isn't a moron. She's your wife" → answer the questions prospects think, at the moment they think them, in their words.
  - **What — Eight Key Elements** (each value element and its opposite): Dream Outcome ↔ *Nightmare* (worst hassles/pain of going without); Perceived Likelihood of Achievement ↔ *Risk* (of not acting; repeating past failures; problems growing) — lower perceived risk by explaining away past failures, success of people like them, authority assurances, guarantees, "at least a better chance"; Time Delay ↔ *Speed* (how slow their current trajectory is vs how much faster with you); Effort and Sacrifice ↔ *Ease* (work/skill needed without you, giving up loves and enduring hates, "work hard and sacrifice a ton right now… and have gotten nowhere"; vs avoid what they hate, do what they love, low skill).
  - **Who — status.** "Humans are primarily status driven"; show how the product changes how others treat the customer. Two groups: the customer gaining status, and status-givers: Spouse, Kids, Parents, Extended Family, Colleagues, Bosses, Friends, Rivals, Competitors. Examples: spouse won't nag about a risk-free purchase; kids notice less stress; competitors' phones stop ringing; buddies say "business must be good" at the golf range. Apply each Who to each value driver → many angles.
  - **When — past, present, future.** Get them to visualize consequences of decision/indecision along their timeline (teased as a kid / can't button jeans / another belt loop), and through others' timelines (kid teased for inherited habits / other dads at practice / doctor says may not walk daughter down the aisle). Then contrast with the good future if they buy.
  - Combination example: spouse (WHO) sees how fast (WHAT) they fit the suit in the future (WHEN); kids month after month (WHEN) tag along to workouts (WHAT); mall reflection in a few months (WHEN) "stuff actually fits me" (WHAT). "The more angles we cover, the more interested they'll become."
  - **Long vs short ads:** the only difference is how many angles you have time to cover; add/remove angles per platform but **keep the callouts (first seconds) and CTAs the same.**
- **3) CTA.** Motivation is huge "for a tiny time." "S-P-E-L-L it out": click this button, call this number, reply "YES," go to this website, scan this QR code. Make it quick and easy: easy numbers, obvious buttons, short memorable web addresses ("acquisition.com/training" not "alexsprivateequityfirm.com/free-book-and-course2782"). [E] He paid $370,000 for the one-word domain Acquisition.com — "everyone else undervalues them." Reuse urgency, scarcity, bonuses from lead-magnet Step 7.

**Step 4 — Get permission to contact them** [S]
- After the action, "Get. Their. Contact. Information." Favourite: a simple landing page — focus on words and image; simpler = easier to test (three templates announced, not in text).
- **Landing page must match the ad** — same look and language, deliver what was promised; avoid a "Frankenstein experience"; "a continuous experience from 'click to close.'"
- **Get more people through more steps** via Cialdini's consistency: remind them of the action just taken and frame the next as aligned ("Now that you just did A, you need to do B to get the most of A"; "Doing A kind of people, do B").
- Not selling yet — asking if they're interested; giving contact info = engaged lead. [E] He avoided building a landing page for four years out of fear; finished before lunch; drag-and-drop tools or cheap freelancers.
- [S] **Conclusion — three core elements of every ad:** (1) Callouts (to notice it), (2) Value elements (reason to act), (3) Calls to action (a way to act).

### #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff (file 020, "buy a dolla' and sell it for two")
- [S] "Efficiency matters more than creativity. **All advertising works.** The only thing that differs… is how well they work." Paid ads = X dollars in, Y dollars out. For a $100M leads machine, "you just need to get it 'good enough' to scale."
- [S] Four questions: How much to spend (three phases); how am I doing (cost & benchmarks); ads not profitable (client financed acquisition); lessons.

**Framework: Three Phases of Scaling Paid Ads** [S]
- **Phase One — Track Money.** Before spending a dollar, set up accurate return tracking ("If you don't track, you're gonna get cleaned out" — casino analogy: play as long as you can afford, not as long as you feel like). Get a consultant/tutorials; "End of story."
- **Phase Two — Lose Money** ("investing in a money printing machine"). You pay first; bank account goes down before up. [E] "I've lost money more times than I've made money running paid ads" — but wins repay all losses and more; losses are frequent but small (he knows when to shut down), wins rare but large (knows when to hit the gas). Illustration: 10 ads × $100 = $1,000; nine lose all; one returns $500 → still down $500; most stop; instead "100x down": $10,000 on the winner → $50,000. "You might lose nine or ninety-nine times in a row before you win big."
  - **Test-budget rule: budget 2× the cash collected from a new customer in the first thirty days (not LTGP) per new ad.** If you know you make $100 in profit from a customer in the first 30 days → let an ad spend up to $200 before shutting it off, *as long as it's getting leads.* **If an ad gets no leads at all, shut it off before spending 1× thirty-day cash ($100).** (He wasted money running ads too long, and lost even more by quitting ads too early; this is the sweet spot.)
  - [E] A business took a year to make paid ads profitable (others in the space ran profitable ads, so it was possible); once profitable, it made the year's "wasted" money back the next month. "Measure the returns over a long time horizon, not next week."
- **Phase Three — Print Money.** If you make back more than you spend: "spend as much as you can" — subject to other business constraints. **Budget method: reverse from customers wanted/handleable, not "how much should I spend."** Ex: can handle 100 customers next month, CAC $100 → $10,000; **pad by 20% because ads get less efficient as they scale** → $12,000 per 30 days → **$400/day.** "Then, I commit to it. If the number terrifies you, then you're doing it right. Trust the data."

**Efficiency benchmark: LTGP : CAC** [S]
- Measure **Lifetime Gross Profit (LTGP)** — "all the money a customer ever spends on your stuff minus all the money it takes to deliver it" — not "LTV." Example: sells for $15, costs $5 to deliver → $10 gross profit; ten purchases → $150 sales, $50 delivery, **$100 LTGP.** Gross profit "is the actual money you use to acquire customers, pay rent, cover payroll."
- **CAC** = cost to acquire a customer. "When I say 'I'm getting 3 to 1' I refer to my LTGP-to-CAC ratio." LTGP > CAC = profitable advertising.
- **The 3:1 observation:** "Every business I invest in that struggles to scale has at least one thing in common — their LTGP to CAC ratio was less than 3 to 1. As soon as I get it above 3 to 1 (either through decreasing CAC or increasing LTGP), they take off. This is a pattern I personally observed, not a rule."
- Two levers: lower CAC (more efficient ads, the Part I steps) or raise LTGP ("a better business model"); "For maximum money… I prefer to do both." Extremes: $1B per customer lets you spend $999M; 1 cent per customer fails even with the best ads.
- [E] From hundreds of entrepreneur conversations monthly: they think they have crappy ads (high CAC) when they have "a crappy business model (Low LTGP)." **"The cost to acquire customers, between competitors in the same industry, is much closer than you'd think. The difference between the winners and the losers is how much they make off each customer."**
- **Diagnostic rule (ads vs business model):** research industry-average CAC. **If your CAC is below 3× the industry average → focus on the business model (LTGP). If above 3× the average → focus on advertising (CAC).** "Costs can only approach zero but how much you make can go up to infinity." Over-optimizing ad efficiency = "trying to 'save your way' to a billion dollars."

**Client Financed Acquisition (CFA)** [S]
- Problem: LTGP > CAC overall, but first-purchase profit is often less than CAC; full LTGP takes months → cash-flow problem cripples ad scaling.
- **Definition:** "if your customer spends more than it costs you to get and fulfill them — in the first 30 days — then you have the funds to scale now and forever." Thirty days chosen because "any business can get interest free money for thirty days in the form of a credit card" — pay off the card, keep the customer, repeat. "Money is no longer your bottleneck. This is the key to limitless scale."
- Worked example: $15/month membership, $5 to deliver → $10 gross profit/month; average member stays 10 months → $100 LTGP; CAC $30 → **3.3:1** — profitable, but $30 out, $10 in, break-even two months later. **Fix: "I immediately sell them more stuff."** A $100 upsell at 100% margin taken by 1 in 5 → +$20 average per customer → $10 + $20 = **$30 gross profit within 30 days = CAC → "free customers"**; the remaining $10/month for nine months is "gravy." "The things you can sell or upsell are unlimited."
- [E] "It's how I've scaled every company I've started for the past seven years past $1M/mo in the first twelve months — without outside funding."
- Bottom line: "Figure out a way to get your customers to pay you back in the first thirty days so you can recycle your cash to get more customers."
- [I] Cross-source: this is the seed of the "Money Models" (2025) material — the 30-day payback/upsell logic is stated here as a paid-ads cash-flow fix.

**Personal lessons from paid ads** [S]
- **Don't confuse sales problems with advertising problems.** [E] A portfolio company spent 12 weeks and $150,000 on ads, got the right leads on the phone, but they didn't buy; owner blamed ads and quit "six inches from gold" — cost "an estimated ~$30M in enterprise value." Rule: **"If your engaged leads have the problem you solve and the money to spend, and they're not buying, then your ads work fine — you have a sales problem."**
- Your best free content makes the best paid ads: a free piece that generates sales or performs very well "nine times out of ten" makes a great ad.
- User Generated Content: customer testimonials/reviews posted publicly; if they perform as free content they often make killer ads; build a system to encourage them — "no extra work."
- "If you say you suck at something, you will probably suck at it" — he said "I'm not techy" for four years; reversed in four hours of effort.
- [S] Action: search "HOW TO PLACE A [PLATFORM] AD," place one for $100 — "you're in the game." Then "Start with an acceptable amount of money you are willing to lose each month. Expect to lose it. You won't be earning, you'll be learning."
- [S] **Why paid ads last:** (1) skills from the other three methods transfer; (2) paid ads cost money — money you'll have from doing the other three first. "Expect to lose more times than we win. And once we win, we scale the hell out of it."

### Core Four On Steroids: More Better New (file 021, "The Size Of The Pie Fallacy")
- [E] Q&A: a chiropractor-niche agency at $2,000,000/year spending ~$30,000/month on Facebook only, no click-to-close tracking, no other platforms, no content, no cold outreach, claimed the niche was saturated ("the $15.1B chiropractor industry"). A second owner in the same niche: "$30k in advertising, across four platforms, last week." Another: $3,000,000/year in weight loss worried that >$40,000/month would saturate a platform with 1B+ daily active users in a $60B industry.
- [S] **Stage flag:** "Typically, they have figured out how to get enough customers from one platform to get them to $1M–$3M per year… it's at this point they hit a wall because they think they can't make more money."
- [S] Two ways to boost any core-four method on your own: **More, Better, New** (used "every time I want to increase the engaged lead flow in a portfolio company").

**More — The Rule of 100** [S]
- "Crank up the volume to your max capacity." Doubling inputs with no improvement still yields more engaged leads. "The biggest increases often come from advertising more."
- **Rule of 100: do 100 primary actions every day, for 100 days in a row.** "I don't make many promises, but this is one… you will get more engaged leads."
- Applied: **Warm reach outs** — 100 reach outs/day (email, text, DM, calls). **Post content** — 100 minutes/day making content; release at least one piece/day on a platform, more as you improve (short/long videos, articles, podcasts, infographics). **Cold reach outs** — 100 reach outs/day (email, text, DM, cold call, flyers); expect lower response, use automation. **Paid ads** — 100 minutes/day making ads (direct-response media ads, direct mail, seminar, podcast spots) and 100 days straight running them at the daily budget calculated in Paid Ads II; "Aim for Client Financed Acquisition."

**Better — testing at the constraint** [S]
- Do more "until it breaks" (CAC too high to sustain), then tweak; keep improvements, toss failures. "Thousands of these tiny tests separate the winners from the beginners."
- **Constraint = the step where the most leads drop off**; smallest improvements there give the biggest boost. Example funnel: 30% opt-in, 5% apply (← constraint), 50% schedule. Improve each by +5 points alone: 30→35% = +16% leads (1.16x); **5→10% = +100% (2x)**; 50→55% = +10% (1.1x). "Focus on the constraint."
- **Cadence: one test per week per platform.** Four reasons: (1) multiple simultaneous tests → you never learn what worked; (2) steps affect each other (more opt-ins but fewer applications); (3) forces prioritization (don't waste the weekly test on red vs bright red); (4) long enough for data, short enough not to waste time (one week is enough for his team size and spend).
- **Testing schedule in every company:** every Monday launch one split test per platform; next Monday (1) pick winners, (2) **log results in a running log of all tests** ("start a zillion improvements later, not at square one"), (3) design the next test to beat the current best. **If you can't beat the current version in four tries (one month), move to the next constraint.**
- Diminishing returns → at some point effort is better spent on "new."

**New — placements, platforms, core four** [S]
- **Size Of The Pie Fallacy:** a small business uses one core-four method, one platform, one way, one targeted audience, sees 3–4 competitors, and assumes that slice is the whole market. "Saying 'I'm as big as I can get' is much easier than saying 'I'm not as good at advertising as I thought.'"
- **When to do new:** "When the returns you get from doing more↔better are lower than what you could get from a new placement or new way of advertising."
- Ordering rule ("what will get me the most leads for the amount of work"), nine times out of ten: **New placements → New platforms → New core four activity.** Placements = other formats on a known platform (Instagram stories/messenger/posts; YouTube shorts/longs/community posts); platforms = e.g. Instagram messenger → Facebook messenger, YouTube shorts → Reels; then an entirely new core-four method.
- Summary sequence: exhaust more–better; new placements on a known platform → known placements on a new platform → new placements on it → add a new core-four activity; then scale with more–better again.
- **First question before investing in a company:** "What's stopping them from doing ten times what they're currently doing?" — sometimes nothing (do more), sometimes something must be made better first.

**Section III conclusion — what to do when** [S]
- "After I do warm outreach to get my pool of customers going — **if I have more time than money, I move to posting content. If I have more money than time, I go with cold outreach or running ads.**"
- "You only need to do one to get engaged leads. So, just pick one. Then, max it out. Do more. Do better. Do new." All combinations compound (content + ads each improve the other; cold outreach + content improves both and works warm leads better).
- [E] Which businesses were built on what: first business — content + warm outreach; gyms — free content + paid ads; Gym Launch — paid ads + cold outreach; Prestige Labs — affiliates; ALAN — paid ads + affiliates; Acquisition.com — posting content. "If you master one, you will be able to feed yourself for the rest of your life."

## SECTION IV: GET LEAD GETTERS

### Section IV intro (file 022, "Give me a lever long enough")
- [S] Leverage = how much you get for the time spent. Lead getters = other people who do the core four on your behalf.
- [S] Four scenarios: #1 you are the lead getter (work HIGH, leads LOW, leverage LOW); #2 you get a lead getter (work LOW, leads LOW, leverage HIGH); #3 you get lots of lead getters — you spend all your time recruiting them (work HIGH, leads HIGH, leverage HIGHER); #4 you get a lead getter who gets lead getters (work LOW, leads HIGH, leverage HIGHEST) — "the makings of a $100M Leads machine."
- [S] Lead getters are not part of the core four because you don't "do" them; you do the core four to *get* them, then they do it for you — "the core four stacks." Lead getters start as leads, become engaged leads, then get others interested. "Ideally, every lead becomes a lead getter."
- [S] Four kinds: #1 Customers (buy, then refer), #2 Employees, #3 Agencies (services that get you leads), #4 Affiliates (businesses that tell their audiences about your stuff).

### #1 Customer Referrals — Word of Mouth (file 023, "The best source of new work is the work on your desk")
- [E] October 2019: sales dipped (15 closes vs 21 the prior week); Facebook had rejected all ads two weeks earlier unnoticed; yet the business "was still doing $500,000 a week from word of mouth alone." At a 700+ gym-owner event ($42,000 each to attend), almost the whole room raised hands as having learned of Gym Launch from another gym owner ("$30M in customers say someone referred them").
- [S] Competitors copied offers, ads, lead magnets, pages, emails, scripts "with little success… the best advertising is a happy customer. An amazing product turns every customer into a lead getter." Products must "not only deliver… but delight."
- [S] **Referral** = a referrer sends an engaged lead to your business; the best come from customers. Referrals = "the lowest cost, highest profit, and best quality leads."
- [S] Referrals grow a business two ways: **worth more (higher LTGP)** — "buy more expensive stuff and buy it more times… tend to pay in cash upfront"; **cost less (lower CAC)** — a referred customer costs nothing.
- [S] **Referral math:** LTGP:CAC of 4:1 means it costs 25% of one customer's LTGP to get another. If every customer brings two more → **12:1**, i.e. just over 8.3% of LTGP per new customer — "three customers for the price of one."
- [S] **Linear vs exponential:** core-four outputs scale roughly linearly with inputs (double reach outs or spend → roughly double leads). Word of mouth: one brings two, two bring four… "It's not linear, it's exponential. Nothing scales like word of mouth."
- [S] **Referral growth equation:** referrals (in) minus churned customers (out). If referrals > churn → you grow without any other advertising; = churn → you need other advertising to grow; < churn → you must advertise just to break even ("most folks"). As percentages: if monthly referral % > monthly churn %, the business compounds every month; core-four-driven growth eventually hits a wall (must spend ever more), referral growth doesn't. [E] PayPal, Dropbox. Small businesses "have about the same customers exiting as they do entering. A hamster wheel of death."
- [S] **Two reasons most businesses don't get referrals:** (1) the product isn't as good as they think; (2) they don't ask.

**Problem #1 — The product isn't good enough** [S]
- "If your product were exceptional, people would already know about it and you'd have more business than you could handle. So if you sell direct to consumers and they are not bringing you more customers, your product has room to improve." Ask: "Why are my customers too embarrassed to tell everyone they know about my product?" — it's "unremarkable, as in — not worthy of remark." [E] His own pool guy, landscapers, cleaners.
- **Goodwill definition:** "Price is what you charge. Value is what they get. The difference between price and value is goodwill." (economists' "customer surplus"). Goodwill → word of mouth → referrals.
- Two ways to build goodwill: lower price (temporary at best; "Any fool can sell something for less" — Rory Sutherland) or **give more value** (the question that matters).

**Framework: Six Ways To Get More Referrals By Giving More Value** (maps to the parts of an ad) [S]
1. **Call Outs → Sell Better Customers.** Customers who get the most value have the most goodwill and refer most. [E] A portfolio PR company for generic small businesses had many sales, high churn, no growth for years. Lowest-churn customers were all in one niche, raising funding from investors — but only 15% of the business; founder feared losing the other 85%. Changed the callouts to that narrow "perfect fit" customer → broke the plateau, "on track to adding millions per month," cheaper leads (more specific messaging), and those customers "started referring like clockwork." Action: find what your most successful customers have in common; target and **sell only** people meeting those criteria.
2. **Dream Outcome → Set Better Expectations.** "Make it better than they expect… you set the expectations." Movie analogy. [E] Early on he "promised everything and the kitchen sink," fulfilment was a nightmare; he inched promises down while keeping quality → room to overdeliver → referrals. **Rule: "Slowly lower the promises you make when making offers. Keep lowering them until your close rates lower. At that point, stop."**
3. **Increase Perceived Likelihood of Achievement → Get More People Better Results.** [E] Gym Launch tracked customer activities (speed to first paid ad, speed to first sale, call attendance) and compared best vs average: **gym owners who ran paid ads and made a sale in the first seven days had 3× LTGP** → they forced everyone to launch ads and sell in the first seven days → average results "skyrocketed," more testimonials and referrals. **Six-step process:** (1) survey customers to find the best results; (2) interview them for what they did differently; (3) find common actions; (4) *force* new customers to repeat those actions; (5) measure improvement in average results (speed and outcome); (6) **match the conditions of your guarantee to the actions that get the best results.**
4. **Decrease Time Delay → Make Faster Wins.** Win = any positive customer experience; faster wins increase perceived speed, stickiness and trust. Give wins more often (a one-week product: one win at the end vs a win every day — "seven times the wins"; seven promises kept lowers the risk of referring). Five practices: deliver seven small things at shorter intervals rather than all at once; **updates are wins** — share progress as often as possible, even "no progress" updates beat silence; **customers form their lasting impression within the first 48 hours after they buy** — force as many wins as possible in that window, "Set many expectations. Meet many expectations. Repeat"; they should always know the next time they'll hear from you — **BAMFAM: Book-A-Meeting-From-A-Meeting**; "Never expect customers to forgive you… deliver early, but never late. **I add fifty percent to my timelines** so I always deliver early."
5. **Decrease Effort & Sacrifice → Keep Making Your Stuff Better.** Six-step monthly loop: (1) use customer-service data, surveys and reviews to find the most common problem; (2) figure out the fix — get feedback from customers who succeeded despite the problem; (3) improve the product; (4) give the new version to a small group of *struggling* customers; (5) get feedback — if solved, roll out to all, else back to step 2; (6) move to the next most common problem, "until the end of time." **"I run this process every month."**
6. **Call To Action → Tell Them What To Buy Next.** "If you don't, they'll still buy… but from someone else." Sell a new thing or more of the same; extends lifetime and goodwill; more things to buy = more things to refer to. [E] A weight-loss company: customers who didn't refer to the first-tier product referred friends to the more expensive product they bought later. People obsess over the front end and neglect the back end; customers who fall off don't refer. Action: "Treat every customer like it's the first time you've sold them. Make sure your next offer is more compelling than your first. Remind them to buy more after each big win."
- [S] **"One Question to Rule Them All"** thought experiment: you've lost all customers but one; the advertising gods ban the core four; all future customers must come from this one customer. How would you treat them — results, onboarding, which customer would you pick? Write it out, then do it. "Start acting like the advertising gods will revoke your core four privileges at any moment."

**Referrals — Ask For Them** [S]
- Businesses have few referrals because they never ask. Epiphany after many failed strategies: **"Asking for referrals only works when you treat it like an offer"** — show the value the customer gets when they refer.
- [E] Dropbox: free storage to both referrer and friend → 39x in fifteen months. PayPal: $10 credit to both → a million users within two years; 100 million six years later; still running.
- Three components of a referral program: how you give the incentive, what you incentivize with, how you ask.

**Framework: Seven Ways To Ask For Referrals** [S]
1. **One-Sided Referral Benefit** — pay your average CAC to the referrer *or* the friend; make them aware. [E] CAC $200: ask for an actual **three-way introduction** (call, SMS, or email — "Not just a name and number"), **right when they buy — don't wait**; then a $200 check when the friend signs up, or $200 off for the friend. Works great for spouses — "Always ask for the spouse & give a household discount."
2. **Two-Sided Referral Benefits** (Dropbox/PayPal model) — pay CAC split: half to referrer (credit or cash), half to friend (credit). [E] $500 programs, CAC $200 → $100 cash to referrer + $100 off for the friend, "good for up to 3 friends" — "worked really well for my local businesses."
3. **Ask For A Referral Right When They Buy** — on the contract/checkout page ask for names and numbers of people they'd like to do this with; show they get better results together. [E] A new salesman shattered event ticket records simply by asking "who else they'd want to have come with them. Then ask them to introduce me" — half his sales were referrals. **Script: "People who do our program with someone else tend to get 3x the results. Who else could you do this program with?"**
4. **Add Referrals As A Negotiation Chip** — if they want $400 and the price is $500, give the discount for an introduction to three friends ("you changed the terms of the sale"). Script: "I can't do anything less than $500 down, but if you make a 3-way text introduction to a few of your friends right now, I'd be happy to cut that initiation fee." If a full-price customer objects: "Stacy got $100 off because she referred three friends. I'm happy to give you $100 if you refer me three friends. Who do you have in mind?"
5. **Referral Events** — points/credits/dollars/bragging rights for bringing friends within an explicit period, typically **one to four weeks**; sell the benefits of working with others with stats. Names: "Bring-a-friend," "Spouse Challenge," "Accountability Buddy," "Coach Challenge" (teams of employees + customers; coaching businesses).
6. **Ongoing Referral Programs** — talk about the benefits of doing things with others all the time (content, outreach, ads). [E] A friend saw a **33% boost in total sign-ups**; 1,000,000 tickets to his virtual event, 250,000 referred.
7. **Unlockable Referral Bonuses** — bonuses for people who (1) refer and (2) leave a testimonial: VIP bonuses, courses, tokens, status, training, merchandise, service levels, premium support, extra hours. Good if you don't like paying cash; can be two-sided since they cost less than cash. "The more insane you make the offer, the more people will refer."
- [S] **Combined promotion example:** give every customer a gift card worth one-third of their program cost to give to a friend who signs up with them; **expiration 7–14 days** forces use; the referrer gains status ("I got this gift card for $2000. Do you want it?" beats "join for $2000 off"); still use the three-way intro; text a picture of the card with the friend's name written on it (a legitimate reason to ask for the name). PS: sell gift cards at **90% off as purchasable gifts (only for friends of customers)** — "you get paid to get new customers."
- [S] Conclusion: referrals are "a way of doing business," not a tactic. Referring is a risk for the customer (goodwill with their friend); they refer when benefits outweigh the risk → add incentives and lower risk by building goodwill (delivering on promises). "I am compensated tomorrow for the value I provide today."
- [S] **Action items:** "Figure out your referral percentages and churn percentages to set a baseline." Implement the six giving-value steps; then use one or more of the seven ways to ask.

### #2 Employees (file 024, "We lose a rep every four weeks")
- [E] June 2021: cold-outreach goal missed two quarters. Diagnosis chain: lose one rep every four weeks (below industry-average churn); hire 1 of 4 candidates HR pushes; HR gets 1 qualified candidate per ~10 screening interviews → **40 interviews per single low-skill frontline hire**; bottleneck = one-on-one screening. Fix: group interviews, screen out "crazies," push anyone with work ethic and basic social skills to sales, "We can teach the rest." Within six weeks hiring outpaced churn; by quarter end cold-outreach sales doubled and were more than half of total sales. "The issue wasn't our cold outreach method, skills, or offer at all. We just didn't have enough people doing cold outreach."
- [S] Lead-getting employees = people you train to get leads the same way you did (ads, content, outreach). More employees → more engaged leads, less of your work — but "employees take work."
- [S] Leverage arithmetic: trade 40 hours of doing for 4 hours of managing (36 hours saved); repeat — 200 hours of work for 20 hours of management; then a manager who costs 4 hours/week to lead → "four hours of work for 200 hours of lead-getting."
- [S] **Why employees make you wealthy:** Scenario #1 — $5,000,000 revenue, $2,000,000 profit, works around the clock → "a high paying job," business worth little. Scenario #2 — same numbers, runs without you → an asset; your time is free; investors (e.g. Acquisition.com) would buy some or all; "$2,000,000 in profit per year, especially if it's climbing, could easily be worth $10,000,000+, right now." "You get rich from what you make. You become wealthy from what you own."
- [S] Beliefs to drop: "If you want it done right, do it yourself"; "No one can do it like I do it"; "Nobody can replace me." He was the one who hired and trained them; "it was my fault they sucked" (no training/leading skills; too poor then too cheap to hire better). Replacement beliefs: "If you want it done right, get someone to spend all their time doing it"; "If I can do it, someone else can do it better"; "Everyone is replaceable, especially me." "Replace yourself as soon as you can."
- [S] **The Internal Core Four (getting employee leads):** Warm outreach → asking your network; Cold outreach → recruiting; Post content → posting job openings; Paid ads → promoting job postings; Customer referrals → employee referrals; Affiliates → associations, guilds, listservs; Agencies → staffing firms; Employees → employees. "When you need more, you do more." You need a reliable process for both customers and employees to scale.

**Framework: The 3Ds of training — Document, Demonstrate, Duplicate** [S]
- Note: some hires already know how to get leads — "count on them to cost more"; starting out you may not afford them → train.
- **Document:** write a checklist of exactly what you do (trusted observers can document; record yourself across multiple shifts and ways). Test: do your next work block following *only* the checklist — "Can you do an A+ job only following your directions exactly?" → first draft.
- **Demonstrate:** do it in front of them step by step; where they stop or slow you, adjust → second draft.
- **Duplicate:** they do it in front of you following the same checklist; if the checklist is right the outcome is the same; if not, "you'll find out fast" — fix the checklist, then have them repeat until right. Standard: "if you vanished tomorrow, could a stranger get the results you get if they only followed your checklist?"
- Training notes: if they get it wrong or are confused, "we got it wrong or made it confusing"; a step needing explanation is too complicated or is several steps; needing long explanations/multiple demos = more work on the checklist (forcing an inferior checklist becomes a nightmare when someone else trains). **Competence ≠ performance** — they may know exactly what to do and not be good yet → "slow then smooth then fast," more reps, don't change instructions. **Judge ability to follow directions more than results** — if they follow directions and get the wrong result, it's the directions. Praise each correct step; when they goof, pause and let them retry; don't take over. If they followed directions exactly and got the wrong result, still praise, fix the checklist on the spot. No punishment during training; reward what you want more of. One piece of feedback at a time; practice until right, then next step. **Whenever there's a major dip from normal performance, retrain the team** — they stopped doing an important step; then reward following it.

**Returns from lead-getting employees** [S]
- Excluding paid-ad spend, the cost of advertising via employees ≈ payroll. **Total payroll / total engaged leads = cost per engaged lead.** Ex: $100,000 / 1,000 leads = $100 per engaged lead; 1 in 10 become customers → CAC $1,000; LTGP $4,000 → **4:1**.
- [E] Acquisition.com at time of writing: ~30,000 engaged leads/month, no paid ads, no outreach; content team ≈ $100,000/month → **≈ $3.33 per engaged lead** in payroll; "We make much more than $3.33 per lead."
- **Which employees to focus on:** if CAC is within 3× industry average → "doing good enough," focus on LTGP. If CAC > 3× industry average → sales problem or advertising problem. **Diagnostic question: "Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend?"** No → not qualified → advertising problem. Yes and buying but not enough of them → advertising problem. Yes but not buying → sales problem. "Don't fire your sales guy if you've got advertising problems. And equally, don't fire your advertising employees if you've got a sales problem."
- Overall rule: total cost of getting a customer should be "at least one-third of the profit you make over the lifetime" [I: i.e. no more than one-third — the 3:1 rule restated].
- [S] Hiring stance: anyone can be taught ground-level jobs — "who you pick is not as important as how you train the ones you do"; "it's about guts," and guts is a learnable skill; for low-level jobs "you'll never have a shortage of labor"; **get picky only for "hyper-specific-multiple-six-figure-C-suite employees" ("fancy employees").** At the current stage: "hire and train anyone willing," then when you find winners, "treat them well, don't burn them out, and give them what they deserve."
- [S] The owner's job: "advertise and sell the vision of your company… publicly and privately to employees and customers alike."

### #3 Agencies (file 025, "Everything is for sale")
- [E] Summer 2016 (five gyms, launching the sixth; couldn't afford agencies on gym margins): asked an agency owner "Can you just show me in a few hours how you would run ads on my account?" → "$750 an hour," first four hours up front (~$3,000). Weekly one-hour calls with homework, recorded and rewatched. Calls 1–2 the agency drove; 3–4 Alex drove; 5–6 "it clicked" (decisions and data tracked); 7–8 he no longer needed help. Total: **"eight hours and $6000 to learn a skill that's made me millions."**
- [S] Agencies = lead-getting service businesses: paid ads, outreach, or packaging/distributing content. Their pitch: already hired and trained people → faster, better, cheaper than DIY.
- [E] **The 11-step agency cycle** he then repeated with "ten plus agencies" used "the right way": (1) excitement about new leads; (2) valuable-feeling onboarding; (3) their "best" senior rep assigned; (4) some results; (5) senior rep moved to the newest customer; (6) junior rep takes over, results suffer; (7) complain; (8) senior rep returns occasionally; (9) results still suffer, cancel; (10) search for another agency, repeat; (11) wonder why results never match the first time.
- [S] **Hiring an agency vs doing it yourself:** "Good agencies cost money. So if you have no money, then agencies are out of the question. You've gotta learn through trial and error." With money, **use agencies for two things: learning new methods and learning new platforms** — they've made the big mistakes; skip to the make-money part; they do early setup/maintenance while teaching you. "Hiring an agency is all about investing in important skills you can't really learn anywhere else." Action: "As soon as you have enough money for a good agency, start poking around."
- [S] **How he uses agencies now — the "hire an agency to learn" opener:** "I want to do what you do in my business, but I don't know how. I'd like to work with you for **6 months** so I can learn how you do it. Plus, **I'll pay extra** for you to break down why you make the decisions you do and the steps you take to make them. Then, after I get a good idea of how it all works, I'll start training my team on it. And once they can do it well enough, I'd like to change to a **lower cost consulting arrangement**. This way, you can still help us if we run into problems. Are you opposed to this?" Most aren't opposed; if not, move on — but negotiate first ("At some price, it's worth it for both of you").
- [E] YouTube: hired **two agencies** — one to keep him committed to making videos and do platform legwork; a second **at 4× the price** to teach the in-depth ideas behind the best content. "Once our videos beat their videos, we dropped down to consulting only." Pattern: "one 'good enough' agency to learn the ropes… then a more elite agency to learn how to maximize it."
- [S] Why it works: better short-term results (they know more) + better long-term results (you/your team learn) + maximum time with their best reps. **You get a fraction of an agency's attention, results worsen as they add clients; your team stays full-time on you.** Rule: "compare your team's results to the agency's until you beat them. Then, cancel the relationship and put the money into scaling everything you just learned." Set terms and deadlines for yourself.
- [S] **How to pick the right agency — ten things the good ones had in common:** (1) someone you know got good results with them (agencies known only via their own ads/cold outreach are probably worse than the word-of-mouth ones); (2) prominent companies got good results; (3) a waiting list (demand > supply); (4) a clear sales process that sets realistic expectations; (5) no short-term hacks, long-term strategy talk, clear timelines for setup, scaling and results; (6) they tell you exactly what they need from you, when, and how they use it; (7) they propose a regular meeting schedule and several update methods; (8) simple-terms updates and clear tracking of costs vs results; (9) a good offer by the value equation (dream outcome matches what you want; how many like you they've gotten there; how long; what they require of you and can you sustain it); (10) **"They are expensive. All good agencies are expensive… but not all expensive agencies are good."** Talk to several even after one agrees.
- [S] Cost caveat: expect a period paying both the agency and your team to do the same work — "breathing room to get results from the agency, learn what they do, and train your team… all at once."
- [E] Time to beat an agency: first about a year; then ten months; then eight; now "less than six months or less" — repeated for every new method or platform.
- [S] **Six next steps:** (1) decide if an agency makes sense now; (2) talk to a lot of agencies to feel the market — "Don't be cheap"; (3) use the agreement framework; (4) set a clear deadline to force you and your team to learn; (5) use both teams until yours beats theirs regularly; (6) switch to discounted consulting "until you feel like you're teaching them instead of them teaching you… then cut 'em loose."

### #4 Affiliates and Partners (file 026, "Nothing makes friends like money")
- [E] Prestige Labs launch, 1 December 2018: >1 year preparation; **$1,000,000+ spent custom-building affiliate software and training; $3,000,000 in inventory** bought before any sales. Two-hour launch presentation "selling" gym owners the opportunity to sell the line (they had to complete training and use it). First three weeks: $150,000 total — at that rate five years to break even, product would expire. Week four: $100,000 Monday, $110,000 Tuesday, $92,000 Wednesday → $450,000+ that week; then $429,000, $383,000, $411,000, $452,000 weekly; **300+ orders/day across 400+ active affiliates**. "I didn't advertise or sell any of the products at all. No paid ads. No sales team… the affiliate machine I built still prints money to this day."
- [S] **Affiliate** = a lead getter that is an independent business telling its audience to buy your stuff. Differs from referrals: they have their own businesses and do their own advertising; they agree to offer your stuff to their engaged leads for money, free stuff, or both. You get affiliates by advertising and making them an offer — but "a unique type of offer": a fast, simple, easy way to make commissions promoting your product. "One of the highest-leverage lead-getters out there."
- [S] **Why an affiliate army:** Scenario #1 — sell 10 customers/month at $10,000 → caps at $100,000/month → $1.2M in 12 months, plateau (low leverage). Scenario #2 — same effort sells 10 affiliates/month, each bringing one $10,000 customer per month → adds $100,000/month every month → **$7.8M in 12 months**, still growing (high leverage).
- [E] **ALAN's three affiliate levels:** (1) agency super-affiliates bringing agencies, (2) agencies bringing local businesses, (3) local businesses bringing end-consumer leads. One super-affiliate → 10 agencies/month → ~50 local businesses/month → ~2,500 leads/month; ALAN worked leads at ~$5 each → $12,500/month per super-affiliate in month one, $25,000 month two, $37,500 month three… → **$1,700,000/month within six months of launch** with only a few super-affiliates.
- [E] Two affiliate-built companies (ALAN, Prestige Labs): **$75,000,000+ revenue from 5,000+ affiliates.**
- [S] Three hard parts: convince them to advertise someone else's stuff; convince them it's yours; keep them advertising.

**Framework: How To Build An Affiliate Army in Six Steps** [S]
1. **Find your ideal affiliates** — "a business with a warm audience full of people like your customers." Questions about your best customers: What do they buy? → who provides it; Where do they go? → what businesses are nearby; What do they like to do? → who provides those services. If direct-to-consumer: their employers (what businesses/jobs). "Who's got my leads!?" [E] For ALAN he listed 200 products/services for agencies and their providers; categories: **software, products, equipment, services, groups they belong to, events they attend** — the start of every new affiliate "hit list." A business in multiple categories = likely lots of leads, great affiliate. Action: build the sheet; search online; or call your customers and ask.
2. **Make them an offer** — same ad structure (callout, value, CTA). Callouts for affiliates: the owners themselves ("ATTENTION SPA OWNERS"), their customers ("Do you work with busy professionals who spend all day in meetings?"), results they promise ("To the heroes who heal the stress of others…"), products/services they deliver ("If you sell lotions or scented oils…"), your own customers ("Do you know anyone who owns a spa?"). **Value template:** "Make more money from your current customers and get more leads than your current offer (dream outcome)… with a high chance of working since your customers already want the product (perceived likelihood)… without needing to build, deliver, or provide customer support for the product yourself (effort and sacrifice)… so you can start selling it tomorrow (time delay)." "Make affiliates the customer you're advertising to."
3. **Qualify them** — they become affiliates when they understand and agree to your terms; set terms to force a fast first win by getting them to **invest** (time, money, and in the product): "nine times out of ten, if they pay, they'll pay attention." Two ways: **Make them a customer** (buy and preferably use the product to keep affiliate status — lowest barrier that worked; "the more money an affiliate invests in your product, the more money they make"; "If they don't believe in your stuff enough to buy it, they probably shouldn't sell it"). **Make them an expert** (pay for onboarding/training that certifies them; product purchase can count as credit toward certification) — covers some advertising cost and funds proper onboarding of "every. single. affiliate." **Price rule: charge 10–20% of what the average active affiliate makes in the first twelve months** (average $40,000/year → charge $4,000–$8,000); too low = not invested, too high = too few affiliates; 10–20% "maximizes the number of people who become active affiliates." Starting out with physical products: bulk-purchasing strategy (pro tip absent from text); otherwise raise the minimum investment every 5 sign-ups (warm-outreach chapter method) until the sweet spot. **Tuning rule: not enough people start → lower the commitment; not enough follow through → raise it.** Favourite: customers *and* experts.
4. **Figure out what to pay them** — biggest problem #1 is buy-in; #2 is keeping them bought in, which depends on rewards. Two questions: *what they get paid for* — first ask "What exactly do I want the affiliate to do?" and pay for that; he pays for two things: new customers and repeat customers; with better tracking, pay for earlier steps (lead magnets downloaded, appointments set) that reliably turn into sales. *How much* — **base it on your maximum allowable CAC.** Example: single-use product sells for $200, costs $40 to fulfil → $160 left for affiliate + business; at a 3:1 LTGP:CAC target, three parts ($120) to the business, one part (**$40 = max allowable CAC**) to the affiliate. **Three-tier payout structure** (he used to give the whole CAC to everyone; now pickier): Tier 1 = 25% of CAC ($10) — anyone who agrees to initial terms (signs up, buys products/certification); Tier 2 = 50% ($20) — once they *activate* (finish the certification, do a set number of posts/outreach, do a launch) — "twice the pay for activating"; Tier 3 = 100% ($40) — once they sustain performance (e.g. maintain five customers per month on subscription). Side effect: average payout below max CAC; [E] if 20% of sales come from tier 1, 20% tier 2, 60% tier 3 → blended payout $30 vs $40 max → LTGP:CAC improves from 3:1 to 4:1; "cutting marketing costs by 33% can translate into 10% to 20% more net profit at the end of the year." Leftover funds contests, affiliate recruitment ads, incentives for rising stars — "or… just plain pocket it."
5. **Get them advertising — Launch.** "Treat them like customers. Give them something good, fast. And nothing does that for affiliates like big launches and lots of cash." Launch mechanics: affiliates advertise your lead magnet or core offer to their audience *before* it's buyable (posting, warm outreach, paid ads, maybe cold outreach) and sell to the assembled engaged leads on launch day (1-on-1, group pitch, or simply make it available). "Good launches have the work done ahead of time. So do all the work for them. Then, they can plug and play." **The Whisper-Tease-Shout method** ("this is how you launch anything, not just affiliates"):
   - **Whisper** (think callouts): curiosity — keep the product mysterious, hint at how big a deal it is, short whispers, show behind-the-scenes. Can start "a few years out": "the longer something appears to take, the more an audience will value it" (ten years to make vs ten days). Embed questions, then say "not yet." [E] Book launch whispers: draft numbers, printed drafts, versions of frameworks, editing videos early morning/late night. **Cadence: whisper every 4–6 weeks until 60 days out; then every 2–3 weeks until 30 days out; then tease.**
   - **Tease** (think value elements): reveal the product, publish the launch date, show value elements using What-Who-When. [E] Book: "hard" information, how the book delivers limitless leads with less work and faster; dozens of usage examples. **Cadence: once per week until 14 days out; then twice per week until 3 days out; then shout.**
   - **Shout** (think CTA): specific actions to take at launch; "pounding the audience with bonuses, scarcity, urgency, and guarantees around being 'the first ones.'" [E] Book: short clear reminders to register; exclusive launch-only bonuses. **Cadence: at least twice a day from 3 days out; on launch day every few hours until 2 hours out; then every 30 minutes until launch.**
   - Action: set affiliates up with everything to run whisper-tease-shout; "They do the advertising. You get the engaged leads. Everyone gets paid."
6. **Keep them advertising — Integration** ("sell an affiliate once and they send engaged leads for life"). Three ways, easiest to hardest:
   - **(1) Affiliates give your lead magnet away with every purchase of their stuff** — makes their core offer more valuable (charge more, more leads) at no cost to them; you upsell your core offer and everything after. Lead-magnet types apply: *Samples and trials* — massage business recruits the personal-training studio next door: every PT buyer gets a free massage. *Reveal a problem* — free/discounted posture assessment with every training package (adds less value to the affiliate offer, but some will), then offer to solve what you revealed. *One step of a multi-step process* — three-part plan (massage, stretching, adjustments): affiliate gives step one free; you upsell the rest. [E] What he did: gym affiliates gave a free nutrition consult to every new member; Prestige Labs upsold products at the consult; gyms could market included consults and charge more.
   - **(2) Affiliates sell your lead magnet** (book, event, service, software, sample) — giving affiliates *all* the cash from a lead magnet you fulfil is "all profit and no work for them"; your money comes from selling the core offer for more than the lead magnet costs to deliver, and you needn't split core-offer revenue. [E] Gyms sold a nutrition consult at $99 or $199 for an hour of Prestige's time and kept the money; Prestige upsold at the consult. Then split money: upfront cash, all cash for a period, or all cash forever — **"I prefer to pay forever so my affiliates stay motivated to keep my customers forever. And, I never cap payouts."**
   - **(3) Affiliates sell your core offer directly** — extra income with no extra work (for some their entire income; offered as a business opportunity or a bolt-on). Affiliate gets a higher share of LTGP; you only deliver. [E] Gyms held nutrition consultations with white-labelled products and upsold supplements to members; revenue split.
   - [E] What worked for them: **Strategy 1 twice per year as a big event + Strategy 3 ongoing**; many similar portfolio businesses use Strategy 2. Bottom line: "Integration is the long term strategy… Treat affiliates like customers. Make your offer make sense for their business."

**Three case studies** [E]
- *Service — national tax-prep/LLC business ($50M):* helps first-time founders (LLCs, bank accounts, articles); doesn't compete with LegalZoom; partners with people who train new entrepreneurs; offers every affiliate's customer a **free LLC setup** — a "high cost lead magnet." Launch: big "blast off" seminar to affiliates' audiences. Integrate: affiliates put the free LLC into their core offer; his team calls those customers and sells what they'll need next (bookkeeping, tax prep). Zero paid ads; true advertising costs = delivering the free LLC + a percentage of every *first* sale to the referring affiliate.
- *Physical products — Prestige Labs:* Gym Launch (community of gym owners with active-adult customers) is the perfect affiliate; new gym owners are introduced to Prestige Labs. Launch: advertising materials for gyms to re-engage current/former customers via warm outreach and free content for a **free 28-day challenge**; gym sells supplements to challengers. Integrate: sell supplements to every new member at a **nutrition orientation** ($50–$1,000 of supplements); a gym signing 20 clients/month at 70% take rate → 14 new customers/month per gym; "4000 gyms × 14 new sales per month × $200 average order = a lot of money every month."
- *Local business — chiropractors (portfolio company teaches this):* target high-volume businesses with people needing adjustments (gyms). Launch: gym promotes a **three-hour workshop** (correct exercise and posture); free or **$29–$99 per person**, split with the gym — "if you give the affiliate 100% of the money, they'll want to do it more"; 30 people × $99 = $2,970 profit to the gym for a few emails and posts; chiropractor soft-pitches, gets a bucket of patients. Integrate: gym includes **one to two adjustments with every new membership** (more valuable membership, signals health/safety focus); every new member = a chiropractic lead. "Repeat this process with thirty gyms, and get more patients than they can handle."

**Costs and returns — affiliate LTGP:CAC** [S]
- Money spent to get an affiliate comes back through the customers they bring; compare the cost to get an affiliate with the gross profit of all customers they send.
- Worked example (widget company): CAC per affiliate $4,000; average affiliate sells $10,000/month for 12 months → $120,000 sales; 75% gross margin → $30,000 COGS → $90,000 gross profit; **40% of gross profit paid to affiliates** → $36,000; leftover $120,000 − $30,000 − $36,000 = $54,000; **$54,000 / $4,000 = 12.5:1.**
- Threshold: "at least at 3:1 to have a decent business"; want higher (5:1, 10:1+). If below 3:1, three fixes: (1) **lower CAC** — get affiliates cheaper (better ads, offer, sales process); (2) **raise LTGP and lower CAC** — get more to activate (a launch process); (3) **raise LTGP** — make them worth more (better integration). Affiliates add at least two customer layers (customers; people who get you customers), super-affiliates a third.
- [S] "Affiliates can't work for my business," the loser said. "I have to make affiliates work for my business," the winner said.
- [S] Two ways to a compounding business: more people who never stop buying (referrals) or more people who never stop selling for you (affiliates). "In theory, once you build an affiliate army, you never need to advertise again." You're "only as good as the goodwill you have with your affiliate partners"; arrange it so they can spend more to acquire customers via a more compelling offer, higher profits, or both.
- [S] **Action steps:** "Advertise your affiliate offer until you get **ten to twenty affiliates**. Get results with those affiliates and use their feedback to work the kinks out of your offer, terms, launches, and integration strategy. Then, scale like crazy by turning their results into your first batch of affiliate lead magnets."

### Section IV Conclusion (file 027, "The last skill you ever need to learn")
- [S] Key strengths: customer referrals — biggest potential for low-cost exponential growth; employees — your direct influence, run the business on your behalf; agencies — teach skills you keep forever and transfer to your team; affiliates — once going, operate entirely on their own.
- [S] **Natural order** (as it tends to happen): first referrals (before a big team) → employees (big team) → agencies (professional help once building a team) → affiliates ("only when a business owner has a handle on managing people inside of their business do they tend to get the guts to try and manage people outside").
- [S] Expectations: "If you think you're going to become a millionaire the first year you go out on your own, you're probably wrong." Pick a strategy and stick with it for a decade; don't quit or switch methods after a few losses. **"I expect to crack a new lead source in three to six months (and this isn't my first rodeo)."**

### Advertising in Real Life: Open To Goal (file 029, "What was your test size?")
- [E] June 2014, first gym: printed **300** flyers, zero leads. Tanning-salon-chain mentor: "I test with **5000**. Then when we find a winner, we put out **5000 per day, every day, for a month**… If we get half a percent, that's decent. If we get one percent, that's a winner. With 300 flyers, half a percent would be like one and a half people." He had done "1/1500th the level of effort required."
- [S] Promise to self: "I would never let effort be the reason anything didn't work for me." "The right action in the wrong amount still fails."
- [S] "Most people dramatically underestimate the volume it takes to make advertising work… doing dramatically less." [E] "I reached out to 100 people over the last six weeks, I only got one customer, it doesn't work" → "You did 1/42 of the amount of work required. It was 100 per day, not 100 over time."
- [S] Advertising is an inputs-and-outputs game: "You input advertising effort. Your output is engaged leads. Period."
- [S] **Open To Goal** ("Rule of 100 on steroids… for the big kids"): [E] a successful gym chain let sales managers set their own schedules provided they signed up **five new members per day no matter what** — done by lunch, leave early; 18 hours, so be it. Definition: commit to the work **until you hit a specific number of outcomes**, not a number of attempts — "It might mean only doing something fifty times… Or… five thousand times, every day, for years." "Give up the idea of 'doing your best.' Instead, do what is required."
- [S] Author's three highest-ROI habits: (1) wake early (4–5 am — "this actually means going to bed early"); (2) get right to work — no rituals, coffee and work; (3) **no meetings until noon.** "A long stretch of uninterrupted work immediately after a long stretch [of] uninterrupted sleep." Sets the daily goal from a known daily capacity; fires and people only after the work block; "working 8 hours straight… my highest ROI 'habit stack'" (more than 12-hour days; throttle back and build up if needed). "Do more than they do, and you will have more than they have." Since his job is usually "get more customers," advertising fills that block; this book was written in it.

### The Roadmap — Putting it All Together: Zero to $100,000,000 (file 031)
- [S] Acquisition.com uses this roadmap "to scale our portfolio companies from a few million a year, all the way to $100,000,000+." Levels identify where you are and what to do next.
- **Level 1 — Your friends know about the stuff you sell.** One offer, one avatar, one platform. Primary action: **warm outreach.** [E] He started by reaching out to everyone he knew.
- **Level 2 — You consistently let everyone you know about the stuff you sell.** You know the exact inputs per engaged lead for your chosen method and get consistent customers by maximizing personal work capacity. Primary actions: **as much warm outreach and posted content as you can consistently.** [E] He maximized with paid ads + a case-study lead magnet, "but looking back, I wish I would have started with posting free content. So I suggest that."
- **Level 3 — You get employees to help you do more advertising.** You've maxed personal inputs, not the platform → do more. Primary action: **hire people to advertise profitably on your behalf.** [E] Hired a videographer and a media buyer.
- **Level 4 — Your product is good enough to get consistent referrals.** Build goodwill; **target 25% or more of customers from referrals**; then ramp advertising again with a more serious team. [E] Ads shut off but referrals kept coming → doubled down: **updated the product from customer feedback every two weeks** and started "a strong referral program with big incentives." Primary actions: focus on the product until referrals are consistent, then scale advertising with a bigger team. **"This is where most people mess up. They let their product slip and never recover."**
- **Level 5 — You advertise in more places in more ways with more people.** First expand to new audiences on the best platform; then all placements and media types on it; once the team gets consistent results, add another platform, lead-getter, or core-four activity. [E] Expanded paid ads to include potential affiliates → paved the way for affiliate programs. Primary action: **advertise profitably using at least two methods on multiple platforms.**
- **Level 6 — You hire killers.** Executives grow departments per method/platform without you; "you're not looking for potential. You're looking for experienced leaders specializing in exactly what you want." [E] "We capped here" at Gym Launch — took three years to learn he needed veteran executives suited to his problems with stronger incentives; by then he'd sold. At Acquisition.com, "expanding the pie to get more of the right people invested in winning" is how they crossed $100M+ then $200M+ portfolio revenue. Primary action: battle-hardened executives and department heads take over new advertising activities and channels.
- **Level 7** — to be written after crossing a billion.

## (b) KEY EQUATIONS / NUMBERS / RULES OF THUMB (flat list with location refs)

**Definitions and core rules**
- Lead = a person you can contact; engaged lead = a person who shows interest; engaged leads are the true output of advertising. (Leads Alone Aren't Enough, file 011)
- Only two ways to grow: more customers; make them worth more. More customers = more, better, cheaper, reliable leads. Double leads → double business, all else equal. (Problem This Book Solves, file 009)
- Core Four = {warm, cold} × {1-to-1, 1-to-many}: warm outreach, post content, cold outreach, paid ads. Not enough leads = not enough skill or volume in the core four. (Section III intro, file 013)
- Try the core offer first; use a lead magnet when people want to know more before buying (more expensive stuff). Lead magnet = complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the problem your core offer solves. (file 012)
- 3 lead-magnet types × 4 delivery methods = up to 12 lead magnets per narrow problem. (file 012)
- Test order for a lead magnet: headline → image → subheadline; can 2x/3x/10x engagement; multiple formats 2–4x take rates. (file 012)
- CTA = what to do + reason to act now (scarcity — ethical/real; urgency; any "because"). "Don't be clever, be clear." (file 012)
- Good lead magnet: engages ideal customers; more engagement than core offer alone; consumed; makes right people more likely to buy. (file 012)

**Lead-magnet money math** (file 012)
- Core offer $10,000 profit; $1,000 ads/call; close 1/3 → $3,000 CAC. Lead magnet: $25 delivery + $75 ads = $100/call (10x more engaged leads); 1 in 10 buys → $1,000 CAC (3x cheaper); 10:1 return; same budget → 3x business.

**Warm outreach** (file 014)
- 100 personalized reach outs/day; follow up up to 3×, once/day for 3 days or until reply; physical mail once/week.
- ACA: Acknowledge, Compliment, Ask. Offer after 3–4 exchanges (messaging/phone) or 3–4 minutes (in person).
- Ask "do you know anybody who…" not "buy"; "five case studies for free"; free in exchange for (1) use it, (2) feedback, (3) review.
- New product/service → first five free. Start charging when referrals start: 80% off next five → 60% → 40% …; +20% every five until sweet spot.
- 1,000 contacts = 10 days of outreach, a month with follow-ups; ≥5 free takers by then.
- Benchmarks: 20% reply; 20% of repliers take free offer; 1 of 4 free → paid ⇒ ≈1 customer per 100 reach outs.
- $104,000/year: 1% of list buys $400; 500 reach outs/week → 5 customers × $400 = $2,000/week × 52.
- 9-word email: "Are you still looking to [4 word desire]?" — no links/images.
- When starting: ≥4 hours/day on getting customers, first thing in the morning.

**Content** (files 015–016)
- Content unit = hook + retain + reward; long form = linked units; "no such thing as too long, only too boring."
- Headline components (use ≥2): recency, relevancy, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing. Retain via lists, steps, stories.
- Give:ask minimum ≈ 3.5:1 (TV 47:13) / 4:1 (Facebook); growing = give far more; "give until they ask."
- Integrated ask example: 3 × 30 s per 60 min = 58.5:1.5. Short platforms → intermittent (e.g. 10 gives then 1 ask); long form → integrated. Unsure → lead magnet ask.
- Depth-then-width caps: short form up to 10 posts/day/platform; long form up to 5 days/week.
- 78% of clients consumed ≥2 long-form pieces before booking a call. 1 in 5 who saw a daily post didn't know about the book.
- Measure monthly: followers, reach, growth rate (new/start). Podcast: 2×/week for 4 years before Top 100; year 5 Top 10 US business.
- Small local business: don't make general business content at first (puddle → pond → lake → ocean).
- Rule of 100 for content: 100 minutes/day, ≥1 post/day.

**Cold outreach** (file 018)
- Build first 1,000 names: software (test a few hundred sample) → brokers (test sample) → elbow grease (freshest; more time than money → start here).
- Personalize with 1–3 facts a friend would know; "blow their minds in under thirty seconds"; give away stuff they actually pay for. Better lead magnet 3x'd take rates.
- Scripts ≤1–2 pages (phone/chat), ≤½ page (email); tweak only after 100 conversations or 10,000 emails.
- Fewer leads → less automation; 10–20% of effort on new tech; email first then follow up other ways; expect 2–3 conversations before a high-ticket sale; restart the list after 3–6 months.
- Phone: 100 calls, 20% pick-up, 25% take → 4 engaged leads (1/hour). Email: 100 sent, 30% open, 10% reply → 3% (target 3%; sample 4%, ~1/3 convert → 1 customer/100). DM: personal video/voice memo → 20% reply.
- Minimum: ≥3× lifetime profit vs cost to get them (portfolio example 30:1).
- Cost model: $15/hr + $50/show, 200 leads/day at $0.10, 2 shows/day → $240/day → $120/show; 33% close → $360/client; $3,600 profit → 10:1. "Add bodies."
- Solve for X: 1 customer/100 emails → 100 customers = 10,000 emails = 333/day = 3 people at 111/day.
- Took ~a year to scale (not 12 weeks); ramp 0→2→4→6→10→14→20→30 sales/month.

**Paid ads** (files 019–020)
- Platform needs 4 things: you use it; you can target; you know its ad format; you have the minimum spend.
- Lookalike list order: customers → warm outreach list → cold outreach leads; add filters; more filters = more efficient, faster burn.
- Ad = Call Out + Value + CTA; focus on the first 5 seconds (20x effect); local callout "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON."
- What-Who-When: 8 elements (4 value elements + nightmare/risk/slowness/effort opposites) × status-givers × past/present/future. Long vs short ads differ only in number of angles; keep callouts and CTAs.
- Landing page matches the ad; consistency framing to get the next step.
- Three phases: Track → Lose → Print. Test budget per ad = 2× cash collected from a new customer in first 30 days (not LTGP); no leads at all → stop before 1× 30-day cash.
- Budget = customers wanted × CAC, +20% pad; e.g. 100 × $100 = $10,000 → $12,000/30 days → $400/day.
- LTGP = lifetime revenue − delivery cost. LTGP:CAC < 3:1 in every struggling portfolio business; above 3:1 they take off (observed pattern).
- CAC < 3× industry average → fix the business model (LTGP); CAC > 3× → fix advertising. Competitors' CACs are close; winners differ in LTGP.
- Client financed acquisition: cash collected within 30 days ≥ cost to get + fulfil (credit-card float). Example: $15/mo, $5 cost, 10 months → $100 LTGP; CAC $30 (3.3:1); $100 upsell at 100% margin taken by 1/5 → +$20 → $30 in 30 days → free customers. Scaled every company past $1M/mo in 12 months without outside funding.
- Engaged leads have the problem and money but don't buy → sales problem, not ads. Best free content → best ads (9/10). Place a $100 ad. Do paid ads last.

**More Better New** (file 021)
- Rule of 100: 100 primary actions/day for 100 days (warm 100 reach outs; content 100 min + ≥1 post; cold 100 reach outs; ads 100 min + 100 days at budget).
- Constraint = biggest drop-off step; 30/5/50% funnel: +5 pts → 1.16x / 2x / 1.1x.
- One test per week per platform; Monday cadence; log every test; 4 failed tries (1 month) → next constraint.
- New when more/better returns < a new placement; order: placements → platforms → core four.
- "What's stopping them from doing 10x?" After warm outreach: more time than money → content; more money than time → cold outreach or ads.
- Stage flag: businesses hit the wall at $1M–$3M/year from one platform.

**Referrals** (file 023)
- Goodwill = value − price. Growth = referrals − churn (>: grow without ads; =: need ads to grow; <: ads to break even).
- LTGP:CAC 4:1 (25% of LTGP per customer) → each customer brings 2 → 12:1 (8.3%).
- Six ways to give value: better customers; lower promises until close rate drops; best-customer actions (ads + sale in first 7 days → 3× LTGP); faster wins (first 48 hours; BAMFAM; +50% timelines; never late); monthly improvement loop; sell them what's next.
- Seven ways to ask: one-sided (pay CAC; 3-way intro at purchase; spouse/household), two-sided ($100 + $100 on $500 program, up to 3 friends), ask at purchase ("3x the results" script), negotiation chip (discount for 3 intros), referral events (1–4 weeks), ongoing program (+33% sign-ups; 250k of 1M referred), unlockable bonuses (refer + testimonial).
- Gift card = ⅓ of program price, expires in 7–14 days; sell gift cards at 90% off to friends of customers. Dropbox 39x in 15 months; PayPal $10/$10 → 1M users in 2 years, 100M in 6.
- Baseline your referral % and churn %.

**Employees** (file 024)
- 40 hours doing → 4 hours managing; 200 → 20 → 4. $2M profit that runs without you ≈ $10M+ value.
- Hiring funnel story: 10 screens → 1 qualified; 4 candidates → 1 hire ⇒ 40 interviews/hire; rep churn every 4 weeks; fix = group interviews.
- Internal core four mapping (network / recruiting / job posts / promoted posts / employee referrals / associations / staffing firms).
- 3Ds: document, demonstrate, duplicate; judge direction-following, not results; retrain on performance dips.
- Cost per engaged lead = payroll / engaged leads ($100,000 / 1,000 = $100; 1/10 convert → $1,000 CAC; $4,000 LTGP → 4:1). Acquisition.com: 30,000 engaged leads/month, ~$100,000 content payroll → ~$3.33/lead.
- Diagnostic: leads have problem + money? No → advertising problem; yes but too few → advertising; yes but not buying → sales. Total acquisition cost ≤ ⅓ of lifetime profit.

**Agencies** (file 025)
- No money → no agency. Use agencies for new methods and new platforms. Opener: 6 months, pay extra for explanations, then lower-cost consulting. Two agencies (second at 4× price). Beat them, then cancel; time to beat: ~1 year → 10 → 8 → <6 months. 10-point picker; good agencies are expensive but not vice versa. First lesson cost $6,000 / 8 hours.

**Affiliates** (file 026)
- Affiliate categories: software, products, equipment, services, groups, events. Scenario: 10 customers/mo → $1.2M/yr vs 10 affiliates/mo → $7.8M/yr.
- Qualify by investment: customer and/or certified expert; charge 10–20% of average active affiliate's first-12-month earnings ($40k → $4k–$8k); not enough starts → lower, not enough follow-through → raise.
- Pay ≤ max allowable CAC (e.g. $200 price, $40 cost, 3:1 → $120 business / $40 affiliate). Tiers 25% / 50% / 100% of CAC on sign-up / activation / sustained performance; blended (20/20/60) $30 vs $40 → 3:1 → 4:1; −33% marketing cost ≈ +10–20% net profit.
- Whisper every 4–6 weeks until 60 days out; every 2–3 weeks until 30 days; tease weekly until 14 days, 2×/week until 3 days; shout ≥2×/day, launch day every few hours until 2 hours out, then every 30 minutes.
- Integration: give away lead magnet → sell lead magnet → sell core offer; pay forever, never cap. Used: strategy 1 twice/year + strategy 3 ongoing.
- Returns: $4,000 per affiliate; $10k/mo × 12 = $120k; 75% margin → $90k GP; 40% payout → $54k → 12.5:1. Need ≥3:1; aim 5:1–10:1+.
- Prestige Labs: 20 clients × 70% = 14 supplement customers/gym/month; 4,000 gyms × 14 × $200. Chiropractor workshop $29–$99, 30 × $99 = $2,970 to the gym; 1–2 adjustments per membership; 30 gyms. ALAN: super-affiliate → 10 agencies → 50 local businesses → 2,500 leads × $5 = $12,500/month, compounding; $1.7M/month in 6 months.
- Start with 10–20 affiliates, then scale.

**Section IV/V rules**
- Natural order of lead getters: referrals → employees → agencies → affiliates. Expect 3–6 months to crack a new lead source. (file 027)
- Set aside 1%, 5% or 10% of ad budget for tests with no expected return. (file 028)
- Flyer test size 5,000 (then 5,000/day for a month); 0.5% response decent, 1% winner; 300 flyers ≈ 1/1500 of required effort; "100 over six weeks" = 1/42 of "100 per day." (file 029)
- Open To Goal = work until N outcomes/day (gym chain: 5 members/day). Habits: up at 4–5 am, straight to work, no meetings until noon, 8 uninterrupted hours. (file 029)
- One-page plan: pick lead type → Rule of 100 or Open To Goal → checklist → do it until you can pay someone → make employees the target → repeat. (file 030)
- Roadmap: L1 warm outreach → L2 warm outreach + content → L3 employees → L4 product/referrals ≥25% of customers (product updated every 2 weeks) → L5 ≥2 methods on multiple platforms → L6 veteran executives → L7 TBD. $100M machine: a third of customers from referrals; 5–10 years if you know what to do; >10 years to the author's first $100M net worth. (file 031)
