## Segment-by-segment notes

### 0. Opening frame (00:00:00–00:00:25)
- [S] Topic: common troubleshooting for people with bad offers, bad lead magnets, or both. He starts with the offer side.

### 1. Commoditised offers: win on speed, risk or ease (00:00:25–00:02:20, "three vectors")
- [S] Condition: if you are in an industry where it is very easy for prospects to compare your offer with someone else's (explicitly "especially true with home services" — kitchen remodelling, roofing, concrete), you must win on at least one of three vectors: **faster**, **less risky**, or **easier** than competitors. Multiple is fine, but focus strategically on at least one.
- [S] Claim: there is always a way to be faster, always a reason to be less risky, always a way to make it easier.
- [S] Objection "I can't afford to do that" → answer: raise prices to reflect the premium, because you are no longer a commoditised product; people will pay for it.
- [S] Speed exercise: ask "what would it take to get this outcome for a customer in one-third the time?" — actually answer it, ideally with your team, instead of saying "we can't".
- [S] Decision rule: if the answer is e.g. paying contractors 50% more to get same-day / next-day response, the next question is "can we sell that to customers?" If yes → "we have a winner". If no → go up-market to wealthier customers "who absolutely value speed, risk and ease".
- [S] Speed is "one of the first places I look" when an offer needs improving.

### 2. Risk vector: insure or guarantee the biggest fear (00:02:22–00:03:00)
- [S] Question: can we insure the biggest fear, or guarantee against it? Three mechanisms: warranties, guarantees, or — his "personal favourite", implemented "with plenty of businesses" — buy an insurance policy against the customer's biggest fear and say "don't worry, we cover damages up to X, because we already have insurance for it".
- [S] Include the insurance cost in your price; it is "typically not nearly as expensive as you think".
- [I] This is the "perceived likelihood of achievement" lever from the Offers (2021) value equation, expressed as an operational tactic; the insurance-policy version is not in the book's guarantee chapter as far as I recall.

### 3. Ease vector: prepare the way for the customer (00:03:10–00:04:00)
- [S] Ease = how much can we prepare for the customer ahead of time: in intake, in onboarding, pre-work done for them so the experience is seamless.
- [S] Observation: so few businesses do this that customers "almost always appreciate and recognise it".
- [E] His own workshops (L1/L2/L3): staff know your name when you walk in and know your business — "all that stuff takes work on our side" to make it delightful.
- [S] These three "value vectors" are "the things that are really going to drive the business"; if you are "suffering because I'm a commodity, this is how you solve it".

### 4. Fourth lever: wrap the current offer in a Money Models attraction offer (00:04:03–00:05:40)
- [S] Introduced "because we have Money Models book out" (author's own cross-reference). You can always wrap what you currently sell in one of: **giveaways**, **win your money back**, **decoy**, **pay less now / pay more later**, or **buy X get Y free**.
- [S] Use case: "if I could just get cheaper leads" → run one of these attraction offer mechanisms.
- [E/S] Giveaway example: a kitchen-remodel giveaway. Then call everyone who did not win the grand prize and offer a second-place / "participation trophy" prize — e.g. "second place is a $2,000 discount on your kitchen. You won. Congrats."
- [S] Fit rules by business type:
  - service-based, where the customer has to do things → **win your money back** "works awesome";
  - more transactional business → **decoy** "works great";
  - **buy-X-get-Y-free** works "in so many different versions".
- [S] Buy-get-free rule: give away more free than the customer pays for, and adjust the price accordingly. [E] Instead of $100/month × 12 = $1,200: "buy six get six" or "buy four get eight" — and make the four $300 each (same $1,200 total).
- [S] Warning: check with legal counsel; advertising laws differ by county and region; make sure the offer is advertising-compliant.
- [I] Direct pointer to Money Models (2025) attraction offers; the "give more free than they pay for, re-price the paid units" mechanic matches that book's buy-X-get-Y-free chapter.

### 5. Big brand ≠ "just give me money": workshop ads vs scaling-roadmap ads (00:05:42–00:08:15)
- [E] Acquisition.com runs two kinds of ads: direct to their Vegas workshops, and to the "scaling roadmap" (a lead magnet he made).
- [E] Finding: direct-to-workshop ads have higher ROAS **up front**; the scaling roadmap has higher ROAS **overall** when the time horizon is expanded — "the part that people miss" — and it **scales better**.
- [S] Reason: a direct "come to a workshop" CTA only reaches the warmest two segments of the stages-of-awareness pyramid (refers to a diagram and to the hooks / GOATed-ads playbooks from earlier in the workshop); the lead magnet reaches "way more people up here than all of this put together".
- [S] Decision rule: if you have higher long-horizon ROAS and can reach more customers, that is "by far the better front end".
- [S] Who says "lead magnets don't work": small businesses ("small is relative — maybe the person you listen to is making $5M a year"). It is not right or wrong: if you only want a smaller amount of money, you will "for sure" get higher ROAS by saying "go buy my stuff". If you want "huge higher realms", you have to soften the ask, make action more attractive, and then build the conversion process around it — "if you have a better dream-outcome match".
- [I] Stage distinction: direct offer = fine for smaller revenue targets; lead magnet + conversion process = required for scale. Compare Leads (2023), which also frames lead magnets as the way to reach people not yet ready to buy.

### 6. "Request a quote" is not a lead magnet (00:08:15–00:08:51)
- [S] Many attendees, "especially you home services guys", run Google ads to a website whose only CTA is "request quote" — "no one wants to request a quote".
- [S] Rule: give something in exchange for contact information. To make it more enticing: give something they actually want, a better dream outcome, described in their own language. [E] "11 frameworks we use to build wildly profitable gyms."

### 7. Three types of lead magnet (00:08:51–00:10:58)
- [S] Types: (1) **reveal a problem**, (2) **trial of the solution**, (3) **one step of a multi-step process** = "splinter offer".
- [E] Reveal a problem: "Seven mistakes homeowners make when fixing their HVAC."
- [E] Trial of solution: a company at a recent L3 whose only service is getting local businesses into position 1, 2 or 3 in local maps ("gym near me"). Their lead magnet: get you into 1/2/3 for free, then call in seven days and ask if you still want it. "Works great… they're crushing it."
- [S] If you can give a trial of the solution, it is "one of the most valuable ways to do it", easy, and yields a ton of leads.
- [E] One step of multi-step: Ashley, fashion consulting (from a "cash episode"). Her CTA was "book a call for an assessment". Replaced with: she determines your colour palette (skin tone, personality, whatever) — the prospect gets real value from the exchange. Next question he asked: "how quickly can you automate it?" — she could, so that became the lead magnet.
- [S] Standard pushback "everything I do comes together" → "No, it doesn't." There are always pieces in a process: you can fix part of the door, put a coat of paint on, apply a sealant, measure it. There is always a piece you can pull out and do "for free or for a significant discount".
- [I] Same three-type taxonomy as Leads (2023) and as transcript Mst4hreQYl0 (which uses the same Ashley example).

### 8. The splinter-offer drawing and behaviour-changing discounts (00:10:58–00:12:35)
- [S] Diagram (reconstructed): an offer with four components A, B, C, D, currently sold for $2,000. Pull B out, give B away for 90% off or free. Once someone has B, they then want to buy A, C, D. "There's almost always a version of this that can get someone in the door."
- [S] Discount rule: he "hates" 10–20% discounts — "no point". A discount must be significant enough to change behaviour; 80–90% does. He can afford it because he applies it to something that costs him very little, and it creates the upsell process into the main thing. Result: cheaper leads.

### 9. Summary (00:12:37–00:13:17)
- [S] Diagnosis: "your offer sucks, which is why you can't get cheap leads" — no promise around speed, nothing around ease, nothing to reverse risk. You do not need all three: "focus on one and hammer it".
- [S] And preceding the offer there is no lead magnet — no good reason to engage with your ads and hand over contact information.
- [S] His favourite lead-magnet rule: give something other people charge for. "The more real the lead magnet is, the more it actually has true cost, the more likely it is that it will work."
