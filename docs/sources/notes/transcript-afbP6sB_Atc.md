### 0. Frame (00:00:00–00:00:40)
- [E] Claim: in 6 months he saved one portfolio company $2M in bottom-line profit. Video promises the problems, the solutions and takeaways to increase customer satisfaction, decrease refunds, get more people to take the next upsell, and decrease chargebacks.

### 1. Problem 1 — onboarding as it existed: five sub-problems (00:00:38–00:03:10)
- [S] Onboarding = what a customer does immediately after they buy. If you have none, adding it will make customers happier, stay longer and pay more — "if you're not doing it, start there".
- [E] Five problems in the existing onboarding:
  1. One-to-many: 25 new customers per onboarding call.
  2. Content broad and company-centred — mission, vision, founders, story — "stuff customers don't care about".
  3. About a third of each call spent resetting individual passwords; the avatar is 55+, low tech proficiency → "way more hand-holding" needed.
  4. Hard pitch of the upsell at the end of the group call.
  5. No next steps for anyone who did not take the upsell — "good luck".
- [E] Mitigating context: a big, successful company; a new product launched to a large existing base produces a huge initial inflow that breaks infrastructure (one rep doing 6–8 calls/day × 25 customers). It does not make sense to hire 100 reps for three days and fire them ("lines out the door at Apple"). Some problems are due to scale and to releasing a new product.

### 2. Principle: the first 24–48 hours frame everything (00:03:10–00:03:50)
- [S] For a service or a software ("very similar"), onboarding is the first real touch point. Customers form their first impression in the 24–48 hours post-purchase; that frames the rest of the relationship.
- [S] Claim: nailing those 24–48 hours increases upsell take-up and how long they stay; adding onboarding "can add two, three months to your LTV if you have any kind of recurring service".
- [S] Warning: if you only send an email with some lists on it, you are missing a massive revenue opportunity.

### 3. Fix design: a 15% pilot, one-to-one (00:03:50–00:04:35)
- [E] They did not "break the entire business yet again": 15% of new customers were routed through a new onboarding process to test whether it beat the current one. Change 1: one-to-many → one-on-one calls.
- [E] Reason for rolling out slowly: no bandwidth to take 25 new customers/hour one-on-one; scaling the team would take a long time; "we definitely weren't going to risk it until we've proven it out".

### 4. Fix: apply the CLOSER "R" (reinforce the decision) to onboarding (00:04:31–00:06:45)
- [S] Change 2: the pilot followed the CLOSER sales framework. After a purchase, the last letter, **R = reinforce the decision**, applies to onboarding.
- [S] How to reinforce: restate the **C, L and O** — **Clarify** why they are there, **Label** them with the problem, **Overview** their past experience — and then sell them again on "the vacation" (what they will experience as a result of having bought).
- [S] Establishing the goal and reminding them of it "is massive".
- [E] ALAN example: same onboarding, only the language changed: "you said you wanted $10,000 a month extra with your agency — why?" → "retire my wife" etc. Relating the next three steps to that stated goal "doubled or tripled — I can't remember the number, it was a lot" the share who followed through.
- [E] Change 3: quick win — a one-on-one concierge set-up of the log-in and a tour of the software (low-proficiency audience). "You want the quick win."
- [E] Change 4: anything that came up was routed live to support and handled on the call. Result: every customer left the call live, logged in, and understanding the basics needed for the goal they signed up for.
- [I] Matches §38 of transcript StVqS0jD7Ls (handshake not handoff, BAMFAM) — same framework from the CS side.

### 5. Pilot result and the value-equation explanation (00:06:45–00:08:40)
- [E] Ascension (taking the paid upsell) went from **20%** under the old process to **48%** in the one-on-one process that restated goals.
- [S] Value equation: dream outcome, perceived likelihood of achievement, time delay, effort and sacrifice.
- [S] Lesson "that has taken me a very long time to learn": how dialled and tight the business is **before you ask for money** is a huge boost to **perceived likelihood of achievement**. If everything promised so far was over-delivered, on time, with quick wins, a goal reminder and one-on-one attention, the next offer feels very likely to be delivered the same way.
- [S] "The best way to get someone to expect value in the future is to provide value today." They did not need to be as sales-heavy because service quality made customers want more.
- [E] Plug: workshops at acquisition.com HQ in Vegas.

### 6. Two universal upsells; buying windows; identity shift (00:08:40–00:11:55)
- [S] If unsure what to upsell on the back of anything, two upsells work "in almost any business": **more of the thing they just bought**, or **more help with the thing they just bought**. Uptake is highest immediately after a first purchase.
- [S] Fear of being pushy → if you believe in what you sell and the customer has the problem, you have "an ethical duty" to present the offer; not hard-close, but make it known.
- [S] Buying is not at regular intervals: someone has a problem, makes all their purchases in a very short period, then almost none.
- [E] Marathon illustration: sign up → shoes → shorts → tank top → arm sleeve → running headphones. The shoe shop that "only sells shoes" loses "Susie" to "above-average Andrew" down the street who sells everything during her "hyper buying frenzy"; "average Adam" offering the case 30 days later is too late. She keeps going to whoever sold her the most.
- [S] Most purchases carry an identity shift ("I'm a runner / author / investor / marketer / healthy now") with associated behaviours and products; enable customers to buy those from you the moment they decide.
- [S] Congruence: if you sell someone on becoming a marathon runner and only have shoes, you evidently did not believe it. "Back it up."

### 7. Fix for "no next steps": notes, tutorials, point of contact — the baton pass / BAMFAM (00:11:55–00:14:33)
- [E] Three additions at the end of onboarding:
  1. **Summary of the call**: announced at the start ("you don't have to take notes, we'll send them") → they are at ease and fully present.
  2. **Tutorials**: "you're especially interested in these three items" → links to those three tutorials in the notes, matched to what they said in the Clarify/Label step.
  3. **Point of contact**: introduced live on the call ("face time") to the person who owns them from now on.
- [S] **Baton pass**: no customer should ever fall into "no man's land". **BAMFAM = book a meeting from a meeting** (credited to a friend, name garbled) — "a way of life": every customer always knows when the next contact is; never end with "we'll coordinate"; both pull up calendars and pick the time.
- [S] Chain: sales rep → onboarding person (with a set time) → support / account rep managing the account ongoing. Three people touch the account, but the customer never has multiple points of contact at once.

### 8. Problem 2 — expectations, mapped onto the value equation (00:14:33–00:16:00)
- [E] Three complaints: (1) "way too techy, I thought it would be easier"; (2) "not enough support, I thought this was hand-held" — in fact more of a DIY product; (3) "even if I'm techy enough and supported, I'm not getting the result soon enough".
- [S] "Very common complaints with any service". Mapping: too techy = too much **effort and sacrifice**; not enough support = **perceived likelihood** of achievement was higher in their head and dropped on contact with reality; taking longer = **time delay**. Together "the nightmare version" of the value equation — three of four drivers gone.

### 9. Fix for complaints 1 and 3: re-script the sales process (00:16:00–00:17:08)
- [S] Two ways to close an expectations gap: add more to the back end (costs money, "and that's okay") or set better expectations up front.
- [E] Listening to sales calls showed phrasings that made the product sound easier and faster than it was. They re-scripted the sales process — and **sold the same amount**; close rates "didn't change at all". The reps were not dishonest; the wording created a different expectation.
- [S] Owners feel they must "promise the moon", but that can make people doubt you; lowering expectations can *increase* perceived likelihood of achievement because the promise becomes believable. You can "reel your sales team back" and still close the same by being clear and honest.
- [I] Connects to transcript StVqS0jD7Ls §14 (sales + CS joint game-tape review to catch expectation-setting phrasings).

### 10. Fix for complaint 2: increase perceived support by making it reachable (00:17:08–00:18:50)
- [E] Support existed but was hard to reach. They added a chat bubble inside the software — no need to leave the product.
- [S] Two types of support: a dedicated account rep (known from onboarding) plus general support for daily small issues — "in your business you probably have similar". This eliminated a *perceived* problem with no new solution, just easier access.
- [E] "201 advanced" bonus: the chat bubble recorded which page the user was on when asking for help → showed where people got stuck → product-roadmap prioritisation. Illustration: if "82% of complaints come from this one point", fix that page before building new features. Proactive (easier page) and reactive (easier support) at once.

### 11. Lower the reading level of everything (00:18:50–00:19:55)
- [E] They lowered the grade level of the language on the page; more people understood and used it.
- [S] Recommendation "across everything": run emails, ads, sales scripts and onboarding scripts through a reading-level grader.
- [S] Claim as heard: "if you're talking to people at a sixth-grade or eighth-grade level then 30% of people are automatically not going to understand" (phrasing ambiguous on whether 6th–8th is the threshold or the target; the point is: go lower); "a huge slice of America doesn't even know how to read"; their money is as good as anyone's.
- [S] Promised effects: improved conversion, experience, expectations, lower refunds.

### 12. Problem 3 — compensation and misaligned incentives (00:19:55–00:24:05)
- [E] Many people were incentivised to sell on the front end; nobody to keep customers on the back end.
- [E] Sub-problem (a): the sales team was fielding support issues because there was no dedicated rep and the sales rep was the person the customer knew.
- [S] Warning: "if you want to destroy your sales team and annihilate goodwill and morale", make them talk to last week's dissatisfied customers while selling new ones. Reps are neither equipped nor incentivised; it eats calendar time; it is out of scope.
- [S] Why it matters: a rep's **conviction** (how they say the words, pace, emphasis, where they lean back) is "one of the strongest predictors of sales close rate". Showing reps the product "sucks" destroys conviction → they stop pushing and stop asking the questions. (Analogy: a new religious convert — no sales skill, but you can feel they believe.)
- [E] Sub-problem (b): the CS team "didn't try" — maybe responded, maybe slowly, no incentive to actually reach the customer. Analogy: a family member in trouble — multiple texts, calls, social media, their friends; not one text and give up.
- [E] Sub-problem (c): when CS did reach someone, calls were unstructured — no script, no process, "what can I help you with?"
- [S] Larger takeaway: every customer conversation has a perfect way it should go. If you script sales, lead nurture and onboarding, script the CS conversations too. Rebuttal to "back-end problems are all different": the same people say "every sale is the same"; sales objections (timing, decision maker, stalls, money) are a known few, and so are CS issues ("can't log in", "don't know step one / two", "this broke") — 90% present with the same few things. "We script."

### 13. Solutions: escalations team, save commission, scripted and practised (00:24:05–00:29:10)
- [E] Solution 1: a **dedicated escalations team** — triage, as in an emergency room (assess severity, route). Effects: close rate goes up, sales morale stays high because they have somewhere to shuttle issues; the team is the catch-all.
- [E] Solution 2: aligned incentives — **10–15% commission, "real money", on the back end for saving customers**.
- [S] Why pay it — the real cost of a lost customer: (1) refund or chargeback; (2) the bigger one "no one talks about": reputation — people buy and tell others not to; you may lose nothing on this sale but lose three (or five) customers you would have had, who tell more people. Also: an unsatisfied customer will not buy your next thing.
- [S] Litmus test: if ads got customers at 10:1 at the start and a year later it is 5:1 or 3:1 — CAC should only rise with CPM ("if CPMs go up 15% a year, that's the amount your CAC should go up"). If CAC doubled or tripled in a short period, [I] the implied cause is reputation. "Everyone believes they're good enough to get referrals; no one believes they're bad enough to get anti-referrals." Counter-move: raise LTV over time to offset CAC rises.
- [S] Save-commission arithmetic: "if I have to pay $200 to save a $2,000 sale, I'd rather have $1,800 than zero — some of you get greedy on this; just do the math".
- [E] Solution 3: **scripted** the escalation call, then **practised** it ("what if they present with problem one… have to talk to their spouse… don't have their card… not a good time"). Now: dedicated team + aligned incentive + scripted process that "yields a consistent result".
- [E] Effect of the commission: CS started texting, emailing and calling **three to five times**.
- [S] **Escalation reach-out is the inverse of lead nurture.** A business is shaped like a bow tie: marketing → lead nurture → sales (narrow) → onboarding → widening out again.
  - Lead cadence (decreasing): 3 calls/day, 3 the next day, a week later once a day, then once every two weeks.
  - Escalated-customer cadence (increasing): email + SMS + call once today; if no response, twice tomorrow; three times the next day — because the issue is "clearly more and more escalated".
- [E] Summary: create the team, align incentives, create the mission → multi-lane, repetitive, escalating reach-out; then a trained script for the conversation.

### 14. Results (00:29:10–00:31:00)
- [E] Net change from fixing onboarding, expectations and incentives:
  1. **61% reduction in cancellations** (wanting to leave, refund, negative experience).
  2. **25% (1 in 4) of people who got on an escalation call chose to ascend** to a higher service level ("I thought I needed a little hand-holding; I want more and will pay for it").
  3. Company-wide **2.4× overall ascensions** (the 20% → 48% figure): almost half of first-purchase customers bought a more expensive version with professional services on top.
- [E] The $2M headline counts only the reductions; ascension revenue is excluded ("most people don't even believe the numbers anyways").
- [S] All of it "drops straight to the bottom line": CAC was already paid, and software has notoriously high gross margins — a higher tier costs "pennies" to deliver.

### 15. Takeaways 1–5 (00:31:00–00:35:40)
- [S] **1. There is always a cost of change.** Do not be discouraged by initial stats after introducing something new — they are the opportunity. "The problems are known and the solutions are known; we just have to do them."
- [S] **2. If you don't track, you don't care.** Asked for CSAT / NPS, businesses say "our refund rate is low". Big difference between people who hate you enough to ask for money back and the mass who dislike you but do not bother (inconvenient, too little money) and tell everyone you suck. [E] At the start this company had almost none of these metrics. Metrics are an intervention in themselves ("weigh yourself every morning and you start to lose weight"); if unsure how to fix something, step one is track it.
- [S] **3. You're never too big for good CS.** [E] Chick-fil-A; Amazon phone support ("good and fast") at their volume.
- [S] **4. Talk to unhappy customers — "that is where the gold is."** Cites Paul Graham (YC): almost every business problem is solved by talking to customers more. Sales → talk to people just sold; product → where people ask questions on your page; also ask "why didn't you buy?"
- [S] Let them vent and many give you more money. Arithmetic: if 1 in 4 buys something 4–5× as expensive, you recover 100% of refunds — [E] $1,000 product, $4,000 upsell, four calls, one buyer → covers the loss on the other three. "It's also a sale."
- [E] Unhappy customers pick up fast — they gave you money and want their value.
- [S] **5. Follow-up is a double-edged sword.** Condition by avatar: low-ticket, high-unit customers "cannot be communicated with too much". The more affluent / expensive the product, the more (counter-intuitively) they do not want reach-out — they just want it to work ([E] "for me with a vendor I never want to talk to them"). If it is a lot of money *to them*, they will want to talk to you all the time.

### 16. Takeaways 6–8 (00:35:40–00:40:37)
- [S] **6. Revenue retention.** Starting out he thought growth = sell more people; "only half true".
- [E] Two-business story: Business 1 sells 100, 200, 300 customers, losing 100% each year → tripled in three years. Business 2 sells 100 each year and keeps them → 100, 200, 300. Same size at year three — but you want Business 2, because without a compounding vehicle the only way to grow is more marketing and selling, and that has a cap.
- [E] Info / direct-response businesses cannot grow past a certain size: "the marketing team of a $300M business" doing $30M because they lose everyone each year.
- [S] Stage distinction: learning to acquire customers is necessary "especially when you're starting out", but "what it took you to get here isn't what it takes to create a really big business".
- [S] As an investor, his number-one metric: how many of last year's customers are still paying — the base to build on; "if we're not keeping anyone, we're just letting more people know we suck".
- [S] **7. Pilots.** They tested with 15% because the intervention was expensive (1:25 → 1:1 = large headcount add). Test with a small slice to check it is worth the investment.
- [S] If results had only marginally improved: not "we shouldn't fix it" but "this isn't the way we fix it". A test is a hypothesis; scientific method: did we do the thing, did the desired result happen. Perfect execution can still fail. Define ahead of time what the stats must look like for the bet to be worth it; if met, roll out.
- [S] Resource-allocation math: a CS rep at $70k–$100k/year who saves $500k–$2M/year = at least 5:1. The bigger the business, the more the owner is an investor allocating people and money to the highest internal return; "if something can get you a 5× or a 10× within the company, you make that bet every time".
- [S] **8. Proactive CS.** Be as fast or faster reaching out to customers as to leads.
- [E] From a *different* portfolio company: the second-highest LTV avatar was customers they had **proactively downgraded** — "you're only using three of our ten things; we have a service level at half the price with the same stuff". Those customers "never cancel"; they see the company as ethical and become a source of word of mouth, referrals and testimonials.
- [S] Owners fear downgrading, but a customer who stays 10 months at half price is worth more than one who cancels in 2 at full price. "Treat the customers as important as you do your leads."
- [E] Outro: pointer to a marketing case study (doubled a portfolio company in 60 days).
