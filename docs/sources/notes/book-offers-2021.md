## START HERE [p10–p12]

- [S] The offer is "the starting point of any conversation to initiate a transaction with a customer." Book's subject: turning advertising dollars into profits "using a combination of pricing, value, guarantees, and naming strategies"; the proper combination = a **Grand Slam Offer** [p10–11].

## SECTION I: HOW WE GOT HERE

### Ch1 — How We Got Here [p16–p23] (origin story)

- [E] Dec 2016: payment processor froze $120,000 of sales for six months; balance $23,036; he paid the salesman's $22,000 commission anyway → $1,036 left [p17–18]. A partner had withdrawn the ~$46,000 proceeds of selling five of six gyms [p20].
- [E] Gym Launch v1: fly to gyms and fill them "using this new methodology that hinged on an offer I had perfected when I owned my chain of gyms" — a "challenge offer", all cash collected up front as his fee [p19–20].
- [E] Launched six gyms simultaneously on a $100,000-limit credit card at $3,300/day ($412/working hour: airfare, hotels, cars, ad spend); January 2017 collected $100,117 — just enough to cover it [p21–22].
- [E] Trajectory: $3,000,000 profit in the first 12 months; $1,500,000+/mo by end of year one; $4,400,000/mo a year later; $120,000,000 sales 24 months after that; at writing, seven eight-figure companies (photography, publishing, fitness, business consulting, beauty; brick & mortar, software, service, e-commerce, training) doing ~$1,600,000/week [p22].
- [S] Attribution: "a girl who believed in me, a credit card, and a Grand Slam Offer." [p22]

### Ch2 — Grand Slam Offers [p24–p32] (what an offer is; the two problems; the author's business model)

- [E] Age 23, $3,000 mastermind seat; organizer TJ's "secret to sales": **"Make people an offer so good they would feel stupid saying no."** Takeaway: "I didn't have to be skilled . . . I just had to come up with things that anyone would say yes to." [p24–25]
- [S] **Definition of an offer:** "the goods and services you agree to give or provide, how you accept payment, and the terms of the agreement." The first thing a new customer interacts with; "the lifeblood of your business" [p26].
- [S] **Offer-quality ladder** [p27]: No offer → no business. Bad → negative profit. Decent → no profit, stagnation. Good → some profit, okay business. Grand Slam → "Fantastic profit. Insane business. Freedom."
- [S] Burgelman: "far better to have understood why you failed than to be ignorant of why you succeeded." Thousands of offers over a decade; most failed; successes followed a repeatable framework [p27].
- [S] **The two main problems** behind all others: (1) not enough clients; (2) not enough cash ("excess profit at the end of the month"). They interlock: acquisition costs come out of margin; prospects compare to cheaper alternatives and the cheapest "wins" (= more work for less); price cuts fill the client load but leave margins thin → "race to the bottom" [p27–28]. Typical models were designed by funded companies that can run at a loss; owners "buy themselves a job" [p28].
- [E] The model is used across chiros, dentists, gyms, agencies, plumbers, roofers, dog walkers, physical products, software, brick-and-mortar [p28].
- [S] "implementing a new offer is about one of the easiest things to do in a business"; even one component adds clients and dollars [p29].
- [S] **Stage flags — reader archetypes** [p29–31]: Archetype I = under $3,000,000/yr — free materials, earn trust. Archetype II = $3M–$10M/yr minimum — equity investment to cross $30M/$50M/$100M+. No coaching/masterminds/courses sold. "getting to $30M, $50M, $100M looks different than getting to $3-$10M" [p30–31]. Four-step model: free value far above market → materials that work → trust of "hyper-executors" at $3M–$10M → invest [p30].
- [E] Every business started since March 2017 hit a $1,500,000/mo run rate; SBA odds of reaching $10M/yr = 0.4% (1 in 250) [p31].

## SECTION II: PRICING — "How to charge lots of money for stuff"

### Ch3 — Pricing: The Commodity Problem [p36–p45]

**Framework: Grow or Die / three ways to grow** [p36–37]
- [S] "Maintenance is a myth." Stock market grows ~9%/yr, so 9% growth = maintenance; in a growing marketplace you may need 20–30%/yr just to keep up [p36–37].
- [S] Only three ways to grow: (1) get more customers; (2) increase their average purchase value; (3) get them to buy more times. Formula: New clients/mo × lifetime value = max monthly revenue. Example: 10 clients/mo × $1,000 LTV = $10,000/mo cap [p37].
- [S] Author Note: strictly there are only two ways — more customers, and increase each customer's value (sub-buckets: profit per purchase; number of purchases). Split into three "because I think it will be easier to understand the money models that will come in Volume III" [p37]. [I] → Money Models (2025).

**Definitions** [p38]
- [S] **Gross profit** = revenue minus the direct cost of servicing an ADDITIONAL customer. Lotion: $10 price, $2 cost → $8 = 80%. Agency: $1,000/mo, $100/mo labor → $900 = 90%. Not net profit (net = after all expenses).
- [S] **Lifetime value (LTV)** = gross profit × number of purchases over the customer's lifetime. Example: $1,000/mo × 90% margin × 5 months = $4,500. Indirect costs (admin, software, rent) excluded. Author focuses on gross-profit LTV and may call it **LTGP (Lifetime Gross Profit)** in other texts [p38]. [I] LTGP is the term used in Leads 2023 / Money Models 2025 (LTGP:CAC).

**Framework: Commoditized vs Differentiated** [p39–40]
- [S] "Commoditized = Price Driven Purchases (race to the bottom). Differentiated = Value Driven Purchases (sell in a category of one with no comparison)." A commodity = "a product available from many places" → if all products are "equal" the cheapest is most valuable by default; the prospect who thinks "these are pretty much the same, I'll buy the cheaper one" has commoditized you [p39].
- [S] Commodities are valued at the point of market efficiency: competition drives price down until margins are "just enough to keep the lights on" — owner becomes "a slave to their business" [p39].
- [S] **GRAND SLAM OFFER — definition** [p39]: "an offer you present to the marketplace that cannot be compared to any other product or service available, combining an attractive promotion, an unmatchable value proposition, a premium price, and an unbeatable guarantee with a money model (payment terms) that allows you to get paid to get new customers . . . forever removing the cash constraint on business growth." Components: (a) attractive promotion, (b) unmatchable value proposition, (c) premium price, (d) unbeatable guarantee, (e) money model / payment terms that get you paid to acquire customers. [I] Component (e) is the thesis of Money Models 2025 (client-financed acquisition); this book only treats it in the agency example and the "implied guarantee" section.
- [S] Effect: "sell in a category of one" / "sell in a vacuum" — the decision becomes "between your product and nothing", so price is whatever value you get the prospect to perceive. Three mechanical outcomes: (1) increased response rates (clicks), (2) increased conversion (sales), (3) premium prices. Same eyeballs cost → more respond, more buy, at higher prices [p40].
- [S] Key point: "a business does the same work in both cases . . . The fulfillment is the same." The Grand Slam Offer makes it *appear* a totally different product, forcing the prospect to stop and re-assess value; "re-calibrate the prospect's value-meter" [p40].

**[E] Real-life money math: agency before/after** [p41–44] (numbers "rounded for illustration" but "based on the real numbers a lead generation agency selling services to brick and mortar businesses experience"; the comparison tables are missing from the extraction)
- Old commoditized offer: "$1,000 down, then $1,000/mo retainer for agency services." At 0.5–1 ROAS you lose money acquiring; 5 customers; break-even after 30 days when they pay another $1,000 each ($10,000 total); month two ($5,000) is the first profitable month, assuming they all stay [p41]. [I] Implies $10,000 ad spend → 5 clients → $5,000 up front.
- Why commoditized: every agency makes the same offer — "You pay us to work. We do work. Maybe you get results from that work. Maybe you don't." Easily duplicated; forces "competitive" pricing; if a client sees a cheaper "same thing" they swap; margins vanish [p42].
- New Grand Slam Offer (verbatim structure): "Pay one time. (No recurring fee. No retainer.) Just cover ad spend. I'll generate leads and work your leads for you. And only pay me if people show up. And I'll guarantee you get 20 people in your first month, or you get your next month free." Plus the full industry playbook free: daily sales coaching for staff, tested scripts, tested price points and offers to swipe, sales recordings, "everything else you need to sell and fulfill." Close: "sound fair enough?" [p42–43]
- Result: same spend, same eyeballs → 2.5× more respond × 2.5× more close × 4× higher up-front price = **22.4× more cash collected up front**; "you spent $10,000 to make $112,000" [p43–44]. Consequence: cost to acquire is so cheap relative to what you make that "your limiting factor becomes your ability to do the work" — cash flow and acquisition stop being the bottleneck [p44].
- [E] This is the exact offer used in the author's software business serving agencies [p44]. Author's own first 18 months: $500k/yr → $28,000,000/yr on under $1M ad spend; "20:1 . . . 50:1 . . . 100:1 returns" [p44].
- [S] Warning: "A grand slam offer given to the wrong audience will fall on deaf ears" → market must be checked first (next chapter) [p44].

### Ch4 — Pricing: Finding the Right Market — A Starving Crowd [p46–p57]

**Framework: Starving crowd** [p46–48]
- [S] Hot-dog stand parable: the one advantage to have is "a starving crowd." "if there is a ton of demand for a solution, you can be mediocre at business, have a terrible offer, and have no ability to persuade people, and you can still make money." [E] Covid toilet-paper shortage: no offer, atrocious pricing, no pitch, rolls sold for $100+ [p47].
- [E] Lloyd's newspaper software: great product, zero-risk revshare offer ("if they made nothing, neither did he"), natural salesman — still declined because the market shrank 25%/yr; he downsized. Pivoted at Covid into automated mask manufacturing → "millions per month" within five months. "Same entrepreneur. Different market." [p47–48]
- [S] "Don't be romantic about your audience. Serve the people who can pay you what you're worth." "We are not trying to create demand. We are trying to channel it." [p48]
- [S] Precondition for the whole book: at least a "normal" market = growing at the same rate as the marketplace, with common unmet needs in one of three categories: improved health, increased wealth, improved relationships. In a dying market "nothing in here would have worked" [p48]. Every market the author has been in was a normal market [p49].

**Framework: Four market indicators** [p49–51]
1. **Massive pain** — they must "desperately need" it. "The degree of the pain will be proportional to the price you will be able to charge." Sales-training line: **"The pain is the pitch."** Articulate the pain accurately and they will almost always buy [p49].
   - Pro tip: "The point of good persuasion is for the prospect to feel understood." [p50]
2. **Purchasing power** — [E] friend with a great resume-improvement system couldn't get paid: his market was the unemployed. Targets must have the money, or access to it, at the prices you require [p50].
3. **Easy to target** — avatars with associations, mailing lists, social groups, channels they all watch. [E] Wanting rich doctors but ads served to nursing students → offer falls on deaf ears (rich doctors are fine; they are easy to find) [p50].
4. **Growing** — tailwind vs headwind. Newspapers had 3 of 4 (pain, money, targetable) but were shrinking [p50–51].
- [S] The three eternal markets: Health, Wealth, Relationships ("always tremendous pain when you lack them"). Method: pick a smaller subgroup inside one of them that is growing, has buying power, and is easy to target [p51]. [E] Relationship coaching: "second half of life" seniors beat college students — more pain (nearer death), more money, easy to find, and more people turn 65 each year than 20 [p51–52].

**Framework: Order of importance — three levers** [p52–53]
- [S] **Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills.** Rate each great / normal / bad. A "great" on a higher-order lever overpowers everything below; "normal" passes the decision to the next lever; "bad" stops the equation unless a "great" above nullifies it.
  - Example #1: great market + bad offer + bad persuasion → still money (hot dogs at 2 a.m. bar close).
  - Example #2 ("most of us"): normal market + Grand Slam Offer → "tons of money even if you're bad at persuasion." This is the book's target case.
  - Example #3: normal market + normal offer → you must be exceptional at persuasion; "the hardest path."
- [S] "you can't be in a 'bad' market, or nothing will work" [p52].

**Rule: Commit to the niche ("niche slap")** [p53]
- [S] Newer entrepreneurs try one offer, don't make a million, conclude "bad market" and hop (dentists → chiropractors). Both are normal markets worth billions: "Either would work, just not both. You must pick one." All markets have unpleasant characteristics; hopping restarts trial-and-error from zero: "you will fail far longer if you keep changing who you market to."

**Framework: Riches in the niches — when to broaden (stage flag)** [p53–56]
- [S] Author Note: "if you are under $10M per year, niching down will make you more money." Beyond $10M it depends on TAM (total addressable market); a business can only grow to meet its TAM; then broaden by going up-market, down-market, or adjacent. $10M/yr is a top-0.4% achievement, so "for 99.6 percent of readers below $10M per year, it's almost always easier to serve fewer clients more narrowly." Many companies reached $30M+/yr in a single niche (chiropractors, gyms, plumbers, solar, roofers, salon owners). **"If you are at $1M or $3M, thinking you have capped and must expand, you are wrong. You just need to be better."** [p54]
- [E] Gym Launch avatar: microgym owner with ~100 members, a signed lease, at least one employee, wanting to help clients lose weight. They turned down (and still turn down) personal trainers and online coaches even though they could help them — for product focus and high-converting messaging [p54].
- [S] "you can literally charge 100x more for the exact same product" by niching (credited to Dan Kennedy) [p54–55].
- [E] **Niche pricing example** [p55–56]: generic "Time Management" course → $19 (maybe $29). "Time Management For Sales Professionals" → $99 (tie to one more deal). "Time Management for B2B Outbound Sales Reps" → $499 (a single sale nets ≥$500; 25× the generic price). "Time Management for B2B Outbound Power Tools & Gardening Sales Reps" → $1,000–$2,000 ("This is made exactly for me"). Same core content; the specificity of application and messaging makes it more compelling and more valuable "in a real way." Parallel: generic weight-loss program $19 vs the same program for shift-nurses $1,997 [p56].
- [S] Positioning template: be "the guy" who solves "this type of problem" for "this type of person" — or more niched: "I solve this type of problem for this specific type of person in this unique counter-intuitive way that reverses their deepest fear." [p56]
- [S] Summary rules: don't pick a bad market; normal is fine; once picked, commit until it works. "If you try one hundred offers, I promise you will succeed." "If your offer doesn't work, it doesn't mean you suck. It means your offer sucks." Keep the market-assessment skill (pain, money, targeting, growth) so that "when lightning strikes, you can make sure it strikes twice" [p56–57].

### Ch5 — Pricing: Charge What It's Worth [p58–p69]

- [E] Gym Lords Summit 2019 — a room of clients paying $42,000/yr; father asked "Is it legal?" Pitch to the father: "If I made you $239,000 extra this year, would you pay me $42,000?" — ~15 hours/week; 11 months; nothing up front ("pay me as you start making the money using the system"). $239,000 = average 11-month topline increase of a gym on the system [p59–60]. Author's take-home >$1,200,000/mo profit for the prior three years [p60].

**Framework: Price-to-value discrepancy** [p61–62]
- [S] People buy to get a deal: they believe VALUE received > PRICE paid. "The moment the value they receive dips below what they are paying, they stop buying from you." Buffett: "Price is what you pay. Value is what you get." [p61]
- [S] Lowering price is the simplest way to widen the gap and "most of the time, the wrong decision." "Getting people to buy is NOT the objective of a business. Making money is." Price can only go down to $0 but infinitely high. Condition: only compete on price if you have "a revolutionary way of decreasing your costs to 1/10th compared to your competition" [p62].
- [S] Kennedy: "There is no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest in the marketplace, but there is for being the most expensive." [p62]
- [S] **Rule of sequence: raise price only after sufficiently increasing value**, so the buyer still gets a great deal — "buying $100,000 of value for $10,000", "money at a discount" [p62].

**The typical (wrong) pricing process** [p63]
- [S] 1) look at marketplace; 2) see what everyone offers; 3) take the average; 4) go slightly below to be "competitive"; 5) provide what competitors offer "with a little more"; 6) end at "more for less." "those competitors they are copying are dead broke." Pricing at market = pricing for market efficiency; over time nobody can give more for less; the bottom 10–20% of operators wash out; new owners repeat the cycle. Goal restated: "we are not trying to get the most customers. We are trying to make the most money" [p63–64].

**Framework: Virtuous Cycle of Price** [p64–66] (diagram missing; text lists the chain)
- [S] When you DECREASE price you: decrease clients' emotional investment; decrease perceived value ("can't be that good if it's so cheap"); decrease client results (not invested); attract the worst clients ("never satisfied until your service is free"); destroy margin needed to deliver an exceptional experience, hire the best, invest in growth/locations/scale [p65].
- [S] When you RAISE price you: increase emotional investment; increase perceived value; increase results; attract the best clients ("easiest to satisfy and actually cost less to fulfill", most likely to receive and perceive value); multiply margin (systems, smart people, customer experience, scale, personal bank account rising even while reinvesting) [p65–66].
- [E] Wine study: same wine served three times at three price labels; tasters rated by price [p66].
- [S] "raising your prices can directly enhance the value you provide." "People want to buy expensive things. They just need a reason." Goal is not slightly above market but so much higher that the consumer thinks "there must be something entirely different going on here" → category of one → "monopoly profits" [p66].
- [S] Condition/warning: if the customer must DO something to get the result, they must be invested; price so "it stings a little"; "Those who pay the most, pay the most attention." Adherence → better results → you genuinely provide more value [p67]. But: "Your product must deliver." Conviction to charge a year's salary comes only from having delivered many times [p67].

**[E] Gym Launch premium-price history** [p67–69]
- Before productizing: flew to 33 gyms in 18 months for full turnarounds; relaunch in 21 days; average +$42,000 in additional sales in 21 days; fee = 100% of the revenue brought in; peak eight gyms/month — a logistical nightmare. One gym owner did the work himself under guidance → ~$44,000 new up-front cash in 30 days (4× his previous month) → proof the process worked remotely → done-with-you model; 4,000+ gyms sold since [p67–68].
- Market prices on entry: low-price full-service marketing $500/mo; one high-price competitor at $5,000. Author priced at 3× the highest and 32× the lowest: **$16,000 for a 16-week done-with-you intensive**; then upsold 35% of buyers into a three-year $42,000/yr agreement [p68].
- Context: average gym owner take-home profit $35,280/yr (half make less) → many committed half or more of annual pay [p68].
- Survey (158 gyms responding, 11 months in program) average improvements: top-line +$19,932/mo (+$239,000/yr); recurring revenue +$13,339/mo (+$160,068/yr); profit $2,943/mo → $8,940/mo (3.1×); +67 clients; monthly churn 10.7% → 6.8%; retail +$4,400/mo; membership prices $129/mo → $167/mo [p68–69].
- [S] Summary: charge a premium so you can do what others can't for clients; "we were charging on a fraction of what our clients made using our system" — the gap between price and value was massive so the cycle kept spinning. "99 percent of businesses need to raise their prices to grow, not lower them. Profit is oxygen." [p69]

## SECTION III: VALUE — CREATE YOUR OFFER

### Ch6 — Value Offer: The Value Equation [p73–p87]

- [S] Opening stance: "the goal should be to charge as much money for your products or services as humanly possible." Abandon "what's fair" — phone companies, pharma, media all charge far above marginal cost. Rule: never charge more than the product is worth, but "charge far more for your product and services than it costs to fulfill it. Think up to a hundred times more, not just two or three times more" — and it should still be a steal for the prospect [p73–74].
- [E] Photography portfolio company: over two years average ticket $300 → $1,500 (5×); profit $1,000/wk → $38,000/wk (38×); less time per customer; higher satisfaction; enabled multi-location scaling; ~$500,000 donated. Method: "figuring out what people valued most, tripling down on it, and ruthlessly eliminating everything else." [p74]

**FRAMEWORK: THE VALUE EQUATION** [p74–77]
- [S] Four drivers; the figure is missing but the text specifies: two on top to INCREASE, two on the bottom to DECREASE, arranged as a division [p75–76].
  1. Dream Outcome (increase)
  2. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (increase)
  3. Perceived Time Delay between start and achievement (decrease)
  4. Perceived Effort & Sacrifice (decrease)
  [I] Standard rendering of the figure: Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice).
- [S] The four map to the father's four questions: "What will I make?" (Dream Outcome); "How will I know it's going to happen?" (Likelihood); "How long will it take?" (Time Delay); "What is expected of me?" (Effort & Sacrifice) [p75–76].
- [S] **Which are hardest / most valuable — "Get the bottom to zero"** [p76]: Early in his career the author focused on the top (dream outcomes, social proof). But "larger-than-life claims are the easiest to establish (and therefore less unique). After all, anyone can make a promise. The harder, and more competitive, are the Time Delay and Effort & Sacrifice." The best companies focus on the bottom: Apple (effortless iPhone), Amazon (one click, near-immediate delivery), Netflix (immediate TV). "the older I get, the more I have shifted my focus to 'the hard stuff' — decreasing the bottom side of the equation."
- [S] Why a division and not a sum: if the bottom = 0, value is infinite regardless of the top — i.e., outcome realised the instant the card is run, with zero effort. Hypothetical limit to strive toward [p76–77].
- [S] **Perception is reality**: value exists only when the prospect *perceives* the increase in likelihood and the decreases in time and effort; actual improvements they don't perceive are "not valuable" [p77]. [E] London Underground: the dotted next-train map (a few million) raised rider satisfaction more than faster trains (billions) [p77].
- [S] Pro tip — Logical vs psychological solutions (Rory Sutherland): logical solutions have usually been tried; look for psychological ones. Pairs: faster trains vs dotted map / models as hostesses; faster elevator vs floor-to-ceiling mirrors; make it cheaper vs make fewer and raise the price. "Any fool can sell a product by offering it for a discount, it takes great marketing to sell the same product for a premium." [p77–78]

**Driver #1 — Dream Outcome (increase)** [p78–80]
- [S] Desires are deep and unchanging; "Our goal is not to create desire. It's simply to channel that desire through our offer and monetization vehicle." Dream outcome = feelings/experiences envisioned; the gap between current reality and dreams; depict it back so they feel understood, then explain how the vehicle gets them there [p78].
- [S] Common wants: to be perceived as beautiful, respected, powerful, loved; to increase status. Many vehicles serve one desire (beauty: make-up, anti-aging, supplements, shapewear, plastic surgery, fitness); beauty itself may be a proxy for status [p79].
- [S] Rule: the dream outcome that most directly increases status is valued most; [E] for many men money > looking handsome because money drives male status. [E] Russell Brunson's wife Collette: minivan over Lamborghini because the minivan raises status among mom friends. "Talk in terms of things your prospect believes will increase their status" [p79–80].
- [S] Pro tip: frame benefits as status gained from others' viewpoint — golf club: +40 yards → "your golf buddies' jaws will drop . . . only you will know." [p80]
- [S] Decision rule: when two offers satisfy the *same* desire, dream outcome cancels and the other three drivers determine perceived value and price: "if two things make someone beautiful, what makes one worth $50,000 and another $5? Answer: The extent of the other three value variables." [p80]

**Driver #2 — Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (increase)** [p80–81]
- [S] The last variable he added; "people pay for certainty." Question in the prospect's head: "How likely do I believe it is that I will achieve the result I am looking for if I make this purchase?" [E] Plastic surgeon's 10,000th patient vs first patient — same service, same time; price differs only by perceived likelihood [p81].
- [S] Communicate likelihood through: messaging, proof, "what we choose to include or exclude in our offer", and guarantees [p81]. [I] Proof/authority is treated only here and in bonus bullet #4; the deeper treatment is in Leads 2023.

**Driver #3 — Time Delay (decrease)** [p81–83]
- [S] = time between buying and receiving the promised benefit; shorter = more valuable. Two elements: **long-term outcome** (what they buy — the dream) and **short-term experience** (what makes them stay long enough to get it). Build in milestones; "We want clients to have a big emotional win early (as close as possible to their purchase)" [p81–82].
- [E] Gym Launch: it takes a while to add $239,000/yr, so the fast win is ads live and first $2,000 sale closed within the first seven days → reinforces the decision, more trust, more compliance [p82].
- [E] Weight-loss clients: introduce them to another member immediately (social benefit) and give a more aggressive diet at the start for a fast emotional win ("backed by science": early victories → persistence) [p82–83].
- [S] Pro tip "Fast Wins": always incorporate short-term immediate wins [p82].
- [E] Liposuction + tummy tuck $25,000 in an afternoon vs people who "barely pay $100/mo to join a bootcamp" — 12–24 months of waiting is the difference [p83].
- [S] Pro tip **"Fast beats free"**: "The only thing that beats 'free' is 'fast.'" Examples: MVD vs DMV (pay $50 to skip the line), FedEx vs USPS, Spotify vs slow free music, Uber vs walking. Decision rule: "if you find yourself in a market competing against free, double down on speed." [p83]

**Driver #4 — Effort & Sacrifice (decrease)** [p83–85]
- [S] = ancillary costs "accrued along the way", tangible and intangible. Fitness vs liposuction comparison (table missing). Plastic surgeons' copy targets exactly these: "Tired of wasting countless hours in the gym . . . tired of trying diets that just don't work?" [p84]
- [E] Selling fitness = "an hour arm-wrestling a client" for 1/10th–1/100th of what they pay for surgery, because likelihood is low and time and effort are high [p84].
- [S] "done for you services are almost always more expensive than do-it-yourself" — effort removed, plus higher perceived likelihood ("if an expert does it") [p85].

**Putting it together** [p85–87]
- [S] Method: score each driver 0/1 and sum for a relative value rating (Meditation vs Xanax, same dream outcome — table missing). Result: Xanax is a multi-billion-dollar product; almost no multi-billion meditation businesses. "that doesn't mean it's perceived as more valuable" [p85–86].
- [E] Supplement industry $123B (Grandview Research) is twice the health-club industry $62B (IHRSA) despite the same perceived objectives, because supplements have lower "costs" (effort/time); people pay $200 for supplements before a $29/mo membership [p86–87].
- [S] Stance: "You can either be right or you can be rich." Use what people value to give them what they need — "monetize the things that people value in order to give them what they really need" [p87].

### Ch9 — Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I: Problems & Solutions [p95–p103]

- [E] Origin: couldn't sell a $99/mo bootcamp ("LA Fitness is $29/mo"); even free trials failed because $99/mo afterwards was "too much" and people "didn't want to start something they wouldn't continue with." Reframed via Dan Kennedy's "irresistible offers": "No one wants a membership; they want to lose weight." [p96]

**FRAMEWORK: OFFER CREATION — STEPS 1–3** [p96–101]
- **Step 1 — Identify Dream Outcome.** [E] "Lose 20lbs in 6 weeks" = big dream outcome + decreased time delay. "I wasn't selling the plane flight. I was selling the vacation." The dream outcome must be them arriving at the destination and what they experience there [p96].
- **Step 2 — List Problems.** Write down everything people struggle with and their limiting thoughts. Method: think about what happens immediately before and immediately after using the product; "What's the 'next' thing they need help with?"; list in the sequence the customer experiences obstacles; "insane detail (the more problems the better!)" [p97].
  - [E] Weight-loss problem list: for each required action (buying healthy food; cooking it; eating it; exercising regularly) the recurring objections: hard/confusing/won't like it/will suck at it; takes too much time; expensive/not worth it; unsustainable — family's needs, travel [p97].
  - [S] Each problem has four negative elements that align with the four value drivers [p98]: Dream Outcome → "This will not be financially worth it." Likelihood → "It won't work for me specifically. I won't be able to stick with it. External factors will get in my way." (author: "the most unique and service-specific of the problem buckets"). Effort & Sacrifice → "too hard, confusing. I won't like it. I will suck at it." Time → "too much time . . . too busy . . . take too long to work . . . won't be convenient."
  - [E] Count: 16 core problems × 2–4 sub-problems = 32–64 problems. "The more problems you think of, the more problems you get to solve." [p98]
- **Step 3 — Solutions List.** Two sub-steps: (a) transform each problem into a solution by asking "What would I need to show someone to solve this problem?" and reversing the obstacle into solution language (beginner shortcut: prefix "how to" and reverse the problem); (b) name the solutions. "You will solve every problem." [p99]
  - [E] Examples [p99–100]: buying food is hard → "How to make buying healthy food easy and enjoyable, so that anyone can do it (especially busy moms!)"; takes too long → "How to buy healthy food quickly"; expensive → "How to buy healthy food for less than your current grocery bill"; unsustainable → "How to make buying healthy food take less effort than buying unhealthy food"; family → "How to buy healthy food for you and your family at the same time"; travel → "How to get healthy food when traveling." Cooking: "How to cook meals in under 5 minutes"; "How eating healthy is actually cheaper than unhealthy food"; "How to make eating healthy last forever." Exercise: "Easy to follow exercise system that everyone enjoys."
  - [S] Repetition across problems is normal because all trace to the four drivers. Warning: "if only one of these needs is missing in a solution, it can cause someone not to buy." [p100–101]
  - [E] Brooke Castillo applied the list to a 90-Day Relationship course (list not in extraction). Takeaway: "Don't be fancy. Just get all the problems down then turn them into solutions." [p101]

### Ch10 — Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim & Stack [p105–p117]

- [S] Author calls this "the meatiest section in the book" and "the most important." Stage rule: **"if this is your first Grand Slam Offer, it's important to over-deliver like crazy. Maybe flying out isn't such a bad idea in the beginning. Make some sales, then think about how to make it easier for your clients."** Target perception: "I get all this, for only that?" — "high prices, but a steal for the value" [p105].

**FRAMEWORK: SALES-TO-FULFILLMENT CONTINUUM** [p106–107]
- [S] Continuum between ease of fulfillment and ease of sales: the less you do, the harder to sell; the more you do, the easier to sell but the harder to fulfill. Goal: the sweet spot — sells very well and is easy to fulfill.
- [S] Mantra: **"Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction."** Generate demand first; get yeses with the offer; only then add friction in marketing or offer less for the same price. Rationale: without demand flowing you can't tell whether the product is good; better to over-deliver with cash coming in than optimize with zero cash and zero feedback [p106].
- [E] Early Gym Launch (done-for-you): 21 days on site; he paid hotels, cars, meals, advertising; generated and worked leads, sold, did first onboarding. Gym owner paid only a $500 refundable deposit → "0 financial risk, 0 time risk, 0 effort"; author kept all up-front cash collected. Solo ~$100,000/mo up-front cash; scaled to a team of eight; it wore everyone out. Teaching instead would earn "maybe a third" per gym but serve hundreds of gyms/month instead of eight. "My promise was fundamentally the same: I will fill your gym in 30 days. It was simply the how and what I did that changed." [p106–107]
- [S] Operating rule: "create cash flow by over-delivering like crazy at first. Then use the cash flow to fix your operations and make your business more efficient" — often without changing the offer, by building systems that deliver the same value at lower cost. "this is how businesses beat one another." [p107]

**FRAMEWORK: OFFER CREATION — STEP 4: Solutions Delivery Vehicles ("The How")** [p108–112]
- [S] "the most important step in this process": list everything you could possibly do to solve each problem — including things you're not willing to do — to push past the default solution. Target reaction: "All that? Seriously? Yes, I'm in." Only needs doing once per product; "You ultimately get paid for thinking." Group ideas by how many people are served at once [p108].
- [E] Problem "Buying healthy food is hard, confusing, and I won't like it" [p108–110]:
  - One-on-one: in-person grocery shopping; personalized grocery list; full-service shopping (100% done for them); in-person orientation (not at store); text support while shopping; phone call while shopping.
  - Small group: group grocery trip; group list-making (one time or weekly); buy and deliver groceries; offsite group orientation.
  - One-to-many: live virtual grocery tour; recorded grocery tour; DIY grocery calculator; predetermined weekly lists per plan; grocery buddy system (pair customers, "takes no time really"); pre-made Instacart carts for one-click delivery.
- [S] Do this for every perceived problem before, during and after the service → "a monster list" [p110].

**FRAMEWORK: PRODUCT DELIVERY "CHEAT CODES" / DELIVERY CUBE** [p110–111] (visual missing; six dimensions)
1. Level of personal attention: one-on-one / small group / one-to-many.
2. Level of effort expected from them: DIY (figure it out on their own) / DWY — done with you (you teach them) / DFY — done for you (you do it).
3. If live, the environment/medium: in-person, phone support, email support, text support, Zoom support, chat support.
4. If recorded, consumption format: audio, video, written.
5. Response speed and window: 24/7; 9–5; within 5 minutes; within an hour; within 24 hrs; which days/hours.
6. **10× to 1/10th test**: "If my customers paid me 10x my price (or $100,000) what would I provide? If they paid me 1/10th the price and I had to make my product more valuable than it already is, how would I do that?"
- [S] Do it per problem — solutions for one problem seed ideas for others. "it's important that you solve every problem. I can't tell you the amount of times one single item becomes the reason someone doesn't buy." [p111]
- [E] Anecdote — eating-out guide [p111–112]: he insisted clients prepare all food at home and lost sales; a business exec said she ate lunch out daily; needing rent money he offered "an eating out guide . . . so you can eat out 100 percent of the time and still hit your goal" → closed. He then built templates/trainings until "there were no more 'one things' to prevent my sales." Rule: "Don't get romantic about how you want to solve the problem. Find a way to solve every problem a prospect presents with." Note: not resolving every obstacle still sells some people, "But you won't sell as many people as you otherwise could have." Goal = "sell the most people, for the highest possible price, with the highest possible margin."

**FRAMEWORK: OFFER CREATION — STEP 5: Trim & Stack** [p112–116]
- **5a Trim** [p112–113]: assess each solution's cost to you. Remove high-cost/low-value first, then low-cost/low-value. Keep: (1) low cost, high value and (2) high cost, high value. Value test via the equation — will this make the person: financially value it; believe they will likely succeed; feel it takes much less effort/sacrifice; reach the result with far less time.
  - [E] Moving in to do someone's shopping, cooking and exercise would make them certain of success but "I am not willing to do that for any amount of money short of a gazillion dollars." Rule: step back one level at a time until you reach a time commitment or cost you can live with — or "massively increase your price so it becomes worth it for you" [p112–113].
  - [S] Priority: "If there's one type of delivery vehicle to focus on, it's creating high value, 'one to many' solutions" — biggest gap between cost and value; high one-time creation cost, "infinitely low additional effort after" ("This is exactly why software becomes so valuable") [p113].
  - [E] Pre-gym online training business: an Excel application that generated 100+ meals matched to macros/calories, exact grocery quantities and bulk-prep instructions; ~100 hours to build; then "truly personalized eating plans for very expensive prices" that took ~15 minutes each [p113]. These meal plans have since been used by 4,000+ gyms and hundreds of thousands of people for "the week or two of dedicated time" [p114].
  - [S] Condition for 1-on-1/small-group: keep high-cost items "for big value adds only"; if a lower-cost alternative achieves the same value, use it. [E] He still does 1-on-1 with portfolio CEOs being scaled past $30M+ [p113].
  - [E] Assets built at his gym: bulking blueprints, eating-out system, travel eating & workout guide, meal plans for every bodyweight and gender, grocery list calculator, plateau-busting meal plans, fast-cooking guides partnered with meal-prep services, plus in-person 1-on-1 nutrition orientations for every client [p113]. One-to-many assets "become the bedrock" of a repeatable, scalable model; the book itself is such an asset [p114].
- **5b Stack — the final high-value deliverable** [p114–116]. Format: *Problem → Solution wording → "Sexier" bundle name (with an ascribed value)*, then the actual delivery vehicles underneath.
  - [E] Weight-loss bundle [p115–116]:
    - Buying food → "how anyone can buy food fast, easy, cheaply" → **Foolproof Bargain Grocery System** ("save hundreds of dollars per month . . . less time than your current shopping routine", $1,000 value): 1-on-1 nutrition orientation; recorded grocery tour; DIY grocery calculator; weekly list per plan; bargain shopping training; grocery buddy system; pre-made Instacart carts; weekly text check-in.
    - Cooking → **Ready in 5min Busy Parent Cooking Guide** ($600 value, "200 hours per year back — that's four weeks of work!"): orientation; meal-prep instructions; DIY meal-prep calculator; weekly prep instructions; meal-prep buddy system; healthy snacks under 5 min guide; weekly post tagging him for feedback.
    - Eating → **Personalized Lick Your Fingers Good Meal Plan** ($500 value; "easier to follow than eating what you used to 'cheat' with and cost less"): orientation; personalized meal plan; 5-min morning shake guide; 5-min budget lunches; 5-min budget dinners; family-size meals; daily meal photo; 1-on-1 feedback meeting to adjust the plan "(and upsell them)".
    - Exercise → **Fat Burning Workouts Proven To Burn More Fat Than Doing It Alone** ($699 value; adjusted "so you never go too fast, plateau, or risk injury").
    - Traveling → **The Ultimate Tone Up While You Travel Eating & Workout Blueprint** ($199 value; no-equipment workouts).
    - Sticking with it → **The "Never Fall Off" Accountability System** ($1,000 value; "works without your permission").
    - Being social → **The "Live It Up While Slimming Down" Eating Out System** ($349 value).
    - **Total value $4,351 — "All for only $599."**
  - [E] Author Note: most of his facilities now sell this bundle for longer periods at **$2,400 to $5,200**; value pieces stack over time, "This is why it is important to begin." [p116]
- [S] The bundle does three things: (1) solves ALL perceived problems, not some; (2) gives *you* conviction that what you sell is one of a kind; (3) makes it impossible to compare or confuse with the business down the street [p117].
- [S] Presentation differs for 1-on-1 vs one-to-many selling — deferred to the bonus chapter [p117]. Summary: the offer is now unique, so prospects "will now only make a value-based rather than a price-based decision" [p117].

**Recap of the five steps (author's own list)** [p114]: 1) dream outcome; 2) list all obstacles ("our opportunities for value"); 3) turn obstacles into solutions; 4) list all delivery vehicles; 5a) trim to highest value / lowest cost; 5b) bundle into the ultimate high-value deliverable.

## SECTION IV: ENHANCING YOUR OFFER

### Ch11 — Enhancing the Offer: intro and "The Delicate Dance of Desire" [p122–p130]

- [E] Charity fundraiser, May 2019: tickets raised from $15,000 to $25,000 and the count cut to 100 on a donor's advice — **"When demand increases, cut supply."** Result: +$1,000,000 before the event started; more-qualified donors; ~$5,400,000 from 100 people ($54,000/head); every auction item one-of-a-kind; bonuses added when two bidders went high so both would donate; items that "wouldn't sell at a different venue for $10,000 sold for $100,000" [p123–124].
- [S] "People want what they can't have. People want what other people want. People want things only a select few have access to." [p124]
- [S] Author Note: other persuasion powers were at play (commitment/consistency, status, peer pressure, goodwill, celebrity endorsement, competition); only scarcity, urgency and bonuses are covered because they belong to the "offer" not "selling" — deferred to "Volume IV $100M Sales" [p124–125].

**FRAMEWORK: SUPPLY / DEMAND — THE DELICATE DANCE OF DESIRE** [p125–129]
- [S] "all marketing exists to influence the supply and demand curve." Increase demand → sell more units; decrease supply → sell each for more. "The 'perfect profit combination' is lots of demand, and very little supply, or perceived supply." Enhancing the offer = increase demand + decrease perceived supply, to sell the same products for more money and in higher volume over a longer horizon [p125].
- [S] Condition (Author Note): "This assumes a regular business who is not trying to gain mass market penetration for some other strategic advantage." [p126]
- [S] Naval Ravikant: "Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want." → we only want what we don't have; to increase demand "we must decrease or delay satisfying the desires of our prospects. We must sell fewer units than we otherwise can." [p126]
- [E] Two-day workshop promo (whisper → tease benefits → shout launch in a week → launch): Scenario one: sell 10 units at $500 (sell the entire pyramid; everyone says yes). Scenario two: sell two 1-on-1 one-day workshops at $5,000 each (skim the top; 80% don't buy) [p126–127].
- [S] **Demand is fractal (80/20), not linear**: "one fifth of the prospects are willing to pay five times the price (or more)" [p127]. In scenario two you make more, have lower costs, provide more value, and leave eight people with unsatisfied desire — which increases their desire further, plus new prospects want in after seeing others "loving it"; next time they act with more urgency and pay more. Next promotion: open three spots at the same price, sell all, still leave demand unmet ("a continuous theme") [p127–128].
- [S] Conversely, re-running scenario one sells fewer each time: "We have no pent up demand. All desire has been satisfied." Pulling the trigger too early → each successive promotion sells less until no sales — "the sad state many businesses find themselves in always trying to generate more demand to make another quick sale" [p128].
- [S] **Hormozi Law: "The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make."** "The longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off." Keep supply (and satisfaction of desire) under the demand you can generate [p128–129].
- [S] Warning both ways: zero supply → no money and eventually people feel rejected ("it takes much longer than you think"); satisfy all demand → "kill your golden goose." "We want the ravenous prospect, not merely the aroused." [p129]
- [S] The five enhancers and their mechanisms [p129]: (1) scarcity — decrease supply to raise prices (and indirectly raise demand via perceived exclusiveness); (2) urgency — increase demand by lowering the prospect's action threshold; (3) bonuses — increase demand (and perceived exclusivity); (4) guarantees — increase demand by reversing risk; (5) names — re-stimulate demand and expand awareness to the target audience. These "outside variables" are "often more powerful than your core offer" [p129].

### Ch12 — Enhancing the Offer: Scarcity [p132–p139]

- [S] Authorities, celebrities and celebrity-authorities charge egregious rates because of **implied demand**: assumed high demand for their time → low supply → must be expensive [p132].
- [S] A rare, must-solve problem has few solvers — often "Supply = 1." Two components compound: rarity of the sources × the actual value provided [p133].
- [E] Many can solve "how do I make $10,000 per month"; far fewer "How can I add $5M in profit without adding any extra product lines?" — a real 60-minute project that produced exactly $5M bottom-line profit by slightly altering the pricing model [p133]. [I] Matches the Castillo testimonial [p5].
- [E] Two $50,000/day offers after a talk (education business stuck at $1M/month; he was doing $1M/week in the same type). Declined — he made more than $50,000/day and didn't want the distraction; later took equity instead of day rates; "for everyone else below that $3M-$10M per year mark, I make all these materials for free" [p133–134].
- [S] "The person who needs the exchange less always has the upper hand." Premium prices are made by "simple supply and demand" [p134].

**FRAMEWORK: SCARCITY** [p134–139]
- [S] Definition: a fixed supply/quantity available creates "fear of missing out", raises the need to act. Mechanism: "Fear of loss is stronger than desire for gain"; humans act more to hoard a scarce resource than to gain a benefit. Aim: buy "in a frenzy, all at once, until you are sold out." [E] Musician's limited-edition hoodie, 100 made, never again [p135].
- [S] **Three types**: (1) limited supply of seats/slots — in general or over X period; (2) limited supply of bonuses; (3) never available again [p135].
- **Physical products** [p135–136]: limited releases (flavors, colors, designs, sizes) — "This month, we are releasing 100 boxes of mint chocolate cookie flavored protein bars." Rules: *always sell out* (better to sell out consistently than over-order); repeat "just not too often" — **once a month** is the sweet spot for companies he knows; *announce that you sold out* (social proof; the fence-sitters desire it more and buy next time). [E] Chanel sends only 1–2 of each piece per store so every item is the last or second-to-last in stock → prices far above market, impulses become purchases.
- **Services** [p136–137] — three ways (pick the one that fits your model):
  1. **Total Business Cap** — "Only accepting X clients at this level of service (on-going)"; creates a waiting list; "The moment the door opens, they jump right in and price resistance disappears." Periodically increase capacity 10–20%, then cap again. Works well for the highest tiers. Example: "My agency only will service twenty-five customers total. Period." Over time raise prices and squeeze out lower-performing accounts, or open slots as capacity allows "always leaving some demand unmet."
  2. **Growth Rate Cap** — "Only accepting X clients per week." Script: "We only accept 5 new clients per week and we already have the first 3 spots taken. I have 6 more calls this week, so you can take the spot or one of my next calls and you can wait until we reopen." Used since the start of his business — you can only onboard so many anyway, so state it.
  3. **Cohort Cap** — X clients per class/cohort on whatever cadence (monthly, quarterly); also gives operational cadence and the sales team "legitimate scarcity." Example: "We take on 100 clients 4 times a year. We open the doors then close them."
- [S] Pro tip — higher-ticket upsells: one-off workshops, trainings, events, seminars, consulting take time and give more access; pair them with clear fixed seat counts. **"always remember have less spots available than you think you can sell"** so that next time everyone remembers you sold out fast — "a compounding strategy that increases in effectiveness over time. One of the few in the marketing arsenal." [p137]
- [E] Scarcity on a free lead magnet: a checklist download limited to 20 new people per week; if it has run out you join a notification list and click immediately when notified; you also consume it more. "we make what would otherwise be a 'neat free download' into a desirable thing not everyone has access to" [p137–138]. [I] Cross-ref: Leads 2023 lead-magnet chapter.
- [S] **Honest scarcity ("the most ethical scarcity")** [p138]: define the number you can actually take in a period and advertise it. "letting people know that you are three-fourths of the way to capacity this week" or "81% to capacity in your total business" moves people. Scarcity implies social proof; "only you get to draw where that line is 'full.'"
- [S] Pro tip — **Extreme scarcity**: sell a very limited supply of 1-on-1 access to you (DM, email, phone, voice memo, Zoom) capped at "a tiny number", priced very high, "then, tell people." "You will make more money than you thought possible"; these tend to be the best clients; choose a delivery medium you don't hate (he hates email/messages, likes Zoom) [p138–139].
- [S] Pro tip — **"Once you're out, you can never come back"**: cap a service level and state that leavers can never return; makes people think hard about leaving. Used at his gyms, in a mastermind, and in the top Gym Lords tier. Condition: "works best with small groups . . . As groups become much bigger, the tactic loses some teeth" [p139].

### Ch13 — Enhancing the Offer: Urgency [p141–p145]

**FRAMEWORK: URGENCY** — "Scarcity is a function of quantity. Urgency is a function of time." Limit *when* people can sign up, not how many; a defined deadline creates urgency; often combined with scarcity [p141]. Four ethical methods:

1. **Cohort-Based Rolling Urgency** [p141–143] (author's favourite; also yields a "choreographed onboarding experience" that matters more as you scale). Even with unlimited weekly starts: "If you sign up today, I can get you in with our next group that kicks off on Monday, otherwise you'll have to wait until our next kickoff date." Juiced version: "I actually had a client who signed up a few weeks ago drop out, so I have an opening for our next cohort that kicks off on Monday. If you are pretty sure you're gonna do this sooner or later, might as well get in on it now so you can start reaping the rewards sooner rather than paying the same and waiting." Rule: the less frequently you start clients, the more powerful (twice a year is very strong); even every other week works.
   - [S] Sidebar "What if I lose sales by turning business away?" — the fear is unfounded. "The biggest sales on a week long campaign or launch happen in the last 4 hours of the last day (up to 50-60%)" — the last 3% of the time creates 50–60% of sales. Those who miss the deadline "were never going to buy" [p142].
   - [S] If a cohort just started and someone wants in: option 1 — offer speedy personalised onboarding as a "bonus" for signing today; option 2 (author's preference) — explain the later start gives them time to review materials, talk to employees (B2B) or family (B2C), and unlock an extended payment plan available only because the start is far out. "you always have the advantage because you are the one who calls the shots" [p143].
2. **Rolling Seasonal Urgency** [p143–144]: real sign-up countdowns on landing pages and copy ("make sure they are real" or you lose credibility); fire up a new campaign/page with new dates each time (~five minutes of editing). Examples: "New Year Promotion ends Jan 30"; "Valentines Lovers Promo Ends Feb 30"; "Sexy By Spring Special Ends March 31"; "Fools in Love April Promo Ends April 30." Same promotion, different seasonal name = a "real" start and finish. "Deadlines drive decisions."
   - [S] Pro tip — **Local businesses: "This is my number one strategy for local businesses."** They must vary marketing more often than national advertisers; a new dated wrapper on the same core service gives urgency and novelty that "will consistently outperform the 'same old' campaigns" [p144].
3. **Pricing or Bonus-Based Urgency** [p144]: the thing they can miss is the promotion/discount/bonus, not the service — lets year-round sellers use urgency honestly. Script: "let's get you started today so you can take advantage of the discount you came in for. I'm not sure how long we will be running it as we change them every 4 weeks or so, and this is one of the better ones we have run in a while." A roofer can't truthfully say he won't serve them after the date, but can time-limit the promotion. Swappable enhancers: pricing promotion, discount, free install, free onboarding, an extra workshop "(valued at $1,000) if they buy now."
   - [S] Pro tip — **Clean your pipeline with every price change**: when raising prices (which he expects readers to do), tell the pipeline "The price is going up! So get in now!" **"Never raise your prices without letting people know."** Shows strength and yields "a nice little influx of cash" from fence-sitters [p144].
4. **Exploding Opportunity** [p144–145]: when the opportunity itself decays with time (arbitrage) — eBay→Amazon arbitrage that self-corrects; crypto; a stock; a new ad platform before competitors; "exploding" job offers whose pay/bonus shrinks daily. Emphasise the decay to force fast decisions.
- [S] Summary: use one or multiple forms; author has used all four "with great effectiveness" [p145].

### Ch14 — Enhancing the Offer: Bonuses [p147–p154]

- [S] Credit to Jason Fladlien. Core claim: **"a single offer is less valuable than the same offer broken into its component parts and stacked as bonuses."** Until you enumerate what you do, it is unknown to the prospect — hence infomercials' "but wait . . . there's more!" [E] Infomercial: one knife for $38.95, then 37 other knives, sharpeners, pans, guarantees [p147–148].
- [S] Mechanism: increase the price-to-value discrepancy by *adding value* instead of cutting price. Anchor the stated price to the core offer, then each bonus widens the gap "until it's too big to bear" [p148].
- [S] Pro tip — **"never discount the main offer. It teaches your customers that your prices are negotiable (which is terrible). Adding bonuses to increase value to close the deal is far superior to cutting prices."** [p148]
- [S] **Presenting bonuses 1-on-1** (group selling is out of scope): ask for the sale first, *before* offering bonuses. If yes → after they sign, reveal the bonuses (wow experience, reinforces decision). If no → present a bonus that matches their perceived obstacle, then ask again; agree with the prospect, add the bonus, ask "Fair enough?"; reciprocity makes repeated additions hard to refuse [p148–149]. Each trim-and-stack deliverable "is now being weaponized and presented at the perfect time" [p149].

**FRAMEWORK: BONUS BULLETS (11 rules)** [p149–150]
1. Always offer them (use the bundle from Section III).
2. Give each a special name with a benefit in the title.
3. Tell them: (a) how it relates to their issue; (b) what it is; (c) how you discovered it / what you had to do to create it; (d) how it will specifically improve their life — faster, easier, less effort/sacrifice (value equation).
4. Provide proof (a stat, a past client, personal experience).
5. Paint a vivid image of life after using it.
6. Always ascribe a price tag and justify it.
7. **Tools & checklists beat additional trainings** (lower effort and time → higher value; "The value equation still reigns supreme").
8. Each bonus should address a specific concern/obstacle about why they can't or won't succeed (prove the belief wrong).
9. Or: what they will logically need next — "solve their next problem before they even encounter it."
10. **The value of the bonuses should eclipse the value of the core offer** — also implies the core must be even more valuable (a bias, not a truth, that you can use).
11. Add scarcity and urgency to the bonuses themselves ("on steroids"):
   - Bonuses with scarcity — v1: only buyers of XYZ program get Bonus #1, 2, 3, "never for sale or available anywhere else"; v2: "I have 3 tickets left to my $5,000 virtual event, if you buy this program you can get one of the last 3 tickets as a bonus."
   - Bonus with urgency — "If you buy today, I will add in XYZ bonus that normally costs $1,000, for free . . . because I want to reward action takers." Distinction: scarcity versions are not time-bound; the urgency version is lost if they don't buy today [p150].

**Advanced: other people's products and services as bonuses (partners/affiliates)** [p151–152]
- [S] Get non-competing businesses to give products/services free in exchange for exposure to your (already-acquired, high-quality) customers. Benefits: goodwill, future referral IOUs, a more valuable offer. "If you secure enough of these relationships, you can literally justify your entire price in the savings and additional true-to-price bonuses."
- [E] Pain-clinic example: massage therapist 1–2 free massages; chiropractor two free adjustments ($100); low-inflammation food company discounts ($50); braces/orthotics discounts ($150); local health club free PT session + free pool month ($100); pharmacy discounts ($100/mo); multiply across providers (ten chiropractors → ten free adjustments). If the offer is $400, the free bonuses alone exceed $400 [p151].
- [S] "Jedi" level: negotiate a group discount for your clients AND a commission to yourself. [E] Prestige Labs (author's sister supplement company): gym-owner clients' sponsored athletes get a 30% discount; the athlete is paid 40% of net sales after the discount; the company gains customers for the commission [p151–152].
- [E] Affiliate commissions negotiated in the pain-clinic example: chiro $100/person; food company free food; orthotics $100/person; health club free membership or $50/person; pharmacy $100/person → the $400 offer can earn an extra $350 "pure profit" from referring customers you already paid to acquire [p152]. Build a Grand Slam Offer with the partner businesses so each bonus itself is de-commoditized [p152]. [I] Cross-ref: Leads 2023 "affiliates" chapter builds this into a lead-getting method; here it is an offer enhancer plus a revenue stream.

**Bonus summary — what to do** [p153–154]
1. Create checklists, tools, swipe files, scripts, templates — anything that takes lots of time/effort to create once but is easy to use and can be given away endlessly.
2. Record every workshop, webinar, event, interview and use recordings as bonuses "as needed to crush a perceived obstacle."
3. Proactively negotiate group discounts and referral commissions with adjacent businesses that solve the customer's *next* need — "you are negotiating with the purchasing power of all your customers at once."
- [S] Author Note: keep a "vault" of bonus assets; information products fit best (high perceived value, low cost, zero operational effort beyond a login); tickets to virtual events; a fixed-cost higher service tier "like giving someone VIP service for a month (which also doubles as a way of upselling them into that level of service . . . more on that in Book II)" [p153–154]. [I] The "Book II" reference here concerns monetization/upsell, which ended up in Money Models 2025, not Leads 2023 — internal cross-reference is inconsistent with the volume numbering elsewhere [p37, p175].
- [S] **What should be a bonus vs core if you fulfil it yourself?** "Wow Factor" — things you wouldn't want someone to miss; pull out the most distinct items that can almost stand alone; especially items "short in length but high in quality or value" (checklists, infographics, e.g. a product launch map) — people won't pay much for them alone but perceive them as very valuable as bonuses [p154].

### Ch15 — Enhancing the Offer: Guarantees [p156–p170]

- [S] "The single greatest objection for any product or service being sold is . . . risk." Reversing risk is "the number one way to increase the conversion of an offer"; spend a disproportionate amount of time on it. [E] Fladlien: conversion 2–4× "simply by changing the quality of the guarantee" [p156, p169].
- [S] Four types: (1) Unconditional, (2) Conditional, (3) Anti-Guarantee, (4) Implied. "You must always hit your guarantee hard, even if you don't have one. Say it boldly and give the reason why." [p156–157]
- [S] **The math** [p157]: if you close 130% as many people and refunds double from 5% to 10%: 100 sales − 5 refunds = 95 net vs 130 sales − 13 refunds = 117 net → 117/95 = 1.23× (23% more, all to the bottom line). For a guarantee not to be worth it, the sales increase would have to be 100% offset by refunds (an absolute +5% sales offset by an absolute +5% refunds — which might mean doubling refunds, unlikely). "Don't be emotional, just do the math."
- [S] **Warning**: "people who buy because of guarantees can become very shitty customers" — may not do the work; tie the guarantee to the actions they must take to succeed [p157].
- [S] Pro tip — **High-cost services**: with high fulfilment cost use a conditional or anti-guarantee, because a refund means eating the refund AND the fulfilment cost [p157–158].
- [S] **Structure that gives a guarantee "teeth"**: "If you do not get X result in Y time period, we will Z." Without the "or what" it is weak (most marketers). Bad: "We will get you 20 clients guaranteed." Better: "You will get 20 clients in your first 30 days, or we give you your money back + your advertising dollars spent with us." [p158]

**FRAMEWORK: GUARANTEE TYPES** [p158–159]
1. **Unconditional** — strongest; effectively a trial (pay first, then see). Gets "a LOT more people to buy" but some refund, "especially as consumer culture continues to shift towards entitlement."
2. **Conditional** — with terms and conditions; "the ones you can get VERY creative on." Aim for "better than money back" to match the client's investment with an equal or higher perceived commitment. If you know the key actions for success, make them the conditions — this improves results. Ideal: 100% qualify but none want it because they got the result; "if given the option of getting a refund or getting the outcome they were promised the vast majority of people will take the outcome."
3. **Anti-Guarantee** — explicitly "all sales are final"; own the position with a creative "reason why" showing massive exposure/vulnerability on your side; "especially important with items that are consumable or massively diminish in value once given."
4. **Implied** — any performance-based offer (revshare, profit share, triggers, ratchets, monetary bonuses): "if I don't perform, I don't get paid"; upside if you over-perform. Condition: only works with transparency for measuring the outcome and trust (or control) over getting paid.

**Stacking guarantees** [p159–160]
- [S] Stack unconditional + conditional: e.g. 30-day no-questions-asked refund, plus a conditional triple-your-money-back at 90 days. Or two conditionals on sequential outcomes: "you'll make $10,000 by 60 days, $30,000 by 90 days as long as you do thing 1, 2, and 3" — future-paces the prospect into a more believable outcome and "shifts the burden of risk back from them onto us."

**Guarantee examples with the author's "take"** (template: "If you don't achieve X, in Y time, we will [insert offer]") [p160–169]
- **[Unconditional] "No Questions Asked" Refund** — client gets: A) full refund; B) 50% refund; C) refund of ad spend and ancillary costs; D) you pay for a competitor's program; E) money back plus $1,000. Take: simplest and "very risky" — accountable even when failure is not your fault; strong but unoriginal; every added condition weakens it. Fladlien's webinar wording is quoted: you can only make "a fully informed decision . . . on the inside"; "whether it's 29 min or 29 days . . . if you ain't happy, I ain't happy"; support answers in 61 min average, 24/7 [p160–161].
- [S] Pro tip — **Name your guarantee**: not "30 Day Money Back Satisfaction Guarantee" but e.g. "if you wouldn't jump into shark infested waters to get our product back, we will return every dollar" or the "Club a Baby Seal Guarantee" [p161].
- **[Unconditional] Satisfaction-Based Refund** — refund at any time if not satisfied with the service level. [E] The author's own weight-loss guarantee: "I'm not guaranteeing you're going to hit this goal in six weeks . . . because I can't eat the food for you. But I am guaranteeing that you will get $500 worth of value and service . . . If you don't feel like we gave you that level of service, I'll write you a check the day you tell me we suck." Paired with a best-case/worst-case close ("Worst case you tell me I suck, I write you a check, and you get six weeks of free training . . . the only thing guaranteed not to help you is walking out of here today"). Result: 2 people took it out of 4,000 sales in 3.5 years [p161–162]. Take: highest form of guarantee; works if you are good at fulfilling — "If not, steer clear." **"I believe this offer works much better in lower-ticket situations. It becomes very risky as you go into higher-ticket services with higher costs of fulfillment."** [p162]
- [S] Pro tip — **Unconditional vs conditional by business type**: "Bigger broader guarantees work better with lower ticket B2C businesses (many people just won't bother taking the time). The higher the ticket, and the more business oriented it is, the more you want to steer towards specific guarantees" (with or without refunds/conditions) [p162].
- **[Conditional] Outsized Refund** — double/triple money back or a no-strings payment of $X,XXX far above price. Take: for high-margin products, always with a consumption condition. [E] Fladlien (who "did $27M in a single day"): "if you buy this course and spend $X on advertising your ecommerce store using the methods herein, and don't make money, I will buy your store from you for $25,000 no questions asked" — a $2,997 course; claimed +$3M in sales; only 10 payouts of $25,000 → $2.75M net extra. Best when the prospect must do a lot and, if done, failure is unlikely; typically outperforms a 30-day money-back in net conversions [p162–163].
- **[Conditional] Service Guarantee** — keep working free until X is achieved. Take: "my personal favorite guarantee of all time" — guarantees the outcome but removes the time element; you never risk the money. Add conditions on key actions (web page set up, attend calls, show up to workouts, weigh-ins, report data). "Since I have been advising businesses to use this particular guarantee, I have yet to have a single person say a client took them up on it." Usually either you happily keep working with a committed client, or it gets dropped because they are close to goal and satisfied; the sales conversation is months old by then [p163–164].
- **[Conditional] Modified Service Guarantee** — another Y-long period free; Y should be at least 2× the original duration. Finite hook; "an easier place for you to start" before the all-out service guarantee [p164].
- **[Conditional] Credit-Based** — refund as credit toward any of your services. Best in an **upsell** to seal a service they're unsure about; worst case they apply credit to what they already like; preserves goodwill [p164].
- **[Conditional] Personal Service Guarantee** — you personally work 1-on-1 free until X. "one of the strongest guarantees in existence . . . a service guarantee on crack"; must add conditions (respond within 24 hours, use the products you specify, etc.). Especially powerful as the owner becomes more "edified." [E] "Alex will personally work with you until your offer converts" would work but be a nightmare → contingencies like: already spent $10,000 on the existing offer using the structure, the offer was for lead generation, and it was a free offer (conditions that make failure unlikely; if they still failed "I could probably fix their problem in ten minutes") [p164–165].
- **[Conditional] Hotel + Airfare Perks** — reimburse product plus hotel and airfare if no value. For workshops/in-person; "like adding an extra $1000 to a guarantee but way more tangible" [p165].
- **[Conditional] Wage-Payment** — pay their hourly rate if a call/session wasn't valuable. If claimed, ask for their tax return and divide by 1,960 working hours; "no one asking for a refund will actually do that" [p165–166].
- **[Conditional] Release of Service** — let them out of the contract free. Powerful if you have enforceable commitments; if you never enforce contracts anyway "you have nothing to lose by adding the guarantee" [p166].
- **[Conditional] Delayed Second Payment** — no second bill until the first outcome (lose first five pounds, make first sale, website live). Best with a systematised first-result process; focuses the team on **activation** (the metric/action that predicts long-term retention). "I've successfully used this guarantee loads of times." [p166]
- **[Conditional] First Outcome** — you keep paying ancillary costs (ad spend, hotel) until the first outcome: "If you don't make your first sale in 14 days, we will pay for your ad spend until you do." Keeps everyone focused on "that first dollar over the bridge" [p166].
- **[Anti-Guarantee] All Sales Are Final** — for exclusive, very valuable things that can't be un-seen or are easy to steal once seen (a checkout-improving line of code, opening messages for dating or cold prospects). Acts as a "damaging admission": "our product is so exclusive and so powerful that once used it cannot be unused." Script: "We are going to show you our proprietary process . . . Our funnels, ads, and metrics . . . as a result, all sales are final." "strong reason why is needed here. Just make one up that sounds compelling." Also works for **high-ticket, high-customisation** work: "If you're the type of customer who needs a guarantee before taking a jump, then you are not the type of person we want to work with. We want motivated self-starters . . ." [p167]
- **[Implied] Performance / Revshare / Profit-share** [p168–169]: Performance: $XXX per sale / per show; $XX per lb lost. Revshare: 10% of top-line; 20% profit share; 25% of revenue growth from baseline. Profit-share: X% of profit / of gross profit. Ratchets: 10% if over X, 20% if over Y, 30% if over Z. Bonuses/Triggers: "I get X when Y occurs." Take: "one of, if not THE most desirable setup" — accountable to results, weeds out low performers, aligns for the long term. **Drawbacks: tracking and collection**; "if you can find a way around that . . . you've hit a gold mine." [E] Taught to agencies using the author's software (retainer → performance wrapped in the Grand Slam Offer): "countless agencies go from $20k/mo to $200k+/mo in a matter of a few months." Variants: pair with a minimum — "the greater of $1000 or 10% of revenue generated"; or "$1000/mo for the first 3 months, then . . . 100% performance" for slow-start setups. Condition: works with quantifiable outcomes; strongest form = no guaranteed payment without performance.

**Create your own guarantee** [p169]
- [S] "Experienced marketers spend as much time crafting their guarantees as the deliverables themselves." Author has used every guarantee above except hotel/airfare and wage-payment. Method: identify the client's biggest fears, pain and perceived obstacles — "What do they not want to have happen if they pay you? What are they most afraid of?" — and reverse them; consider time, emotion and outside costs; "The more specific and creative the guarantee is, the better."
- [S] Warning: guarantees are enhancers, "they cannot make a business. If a guarantee is used to cover up a poor sales team or a poor product, it will backfire into lots of refunds."
- [S] **Sequencing advice (stage rule)**: "Start selling service-based guarantees or setting up performance partnerships. This will make all sales final (so no fear from refunds). Most importantly, it will commit you to your customers' results and keep you honest. From there, either keep that guarantee and scale (perfectly fine), or move up the food chain to less restrictive guarantees to increase volume." [p169]

### Ch16 — Enhancing the Offer: Naming [p172–p181]

- [S] Epigraph: implicit-egotism effect — we are drawn to things that resemble us. A Grand Slam Offer makes no money if no one finds out; naming determines ad conversion, response to outbound emails/cold calls/texts, and inbound organic responses [p169, p172]. [E] "Free Six-Week Stress Release Challenge" vs "Float Tank Center Session" — same thing, first gets far more response [p172].
- [S] Offers fatigue over time, faster locally: reaching 1,000 people costs ~$20 on most platforms, so 200,000 people in an addressable area ≈ $10,000 to reach everyone once. Disclaimer: one exposure ≠ fatigue — most people don't notice an offer on first mention; keep making new creative (videos, images), hooks, stories and copy around the same offer; fatigue is a matter of years, not months [p172–173].
- [S] Renaming refreshes an offer without changing it: "We are only changing the wrapping paper." "This one concept will get you leads forever." [p173]

**FRAMEWORK: M-A-G-I-C NAMING FORMULA** [p173–176] (figure missing)
- [S] Use typically **three to five** of the five components; all five usually makes the name too long; "balance between brevity and specificity"; the only way to know is to write names out and test. Theory mapping: M-Magnet = Attention; A-Avatar = Discrimination; G-Goal = Purpose; I-Interval = Timeline; C-Container = Method [p174]. Order need not be M-A-G-I-C — "Do what sounds punchier" [p178].
- **M — Make a Magnetic "Reason Why"**: a word/phrase telling why you run the promotion; "think like a fraternity party planner" (a party because someone had wisdom teeth out); must answer "Why are they making this great offer?" and/or "Why should I respond? What's in it for me?" Examples: Free, 88% off, Giveaway, Spring, Summer, Back To School, Grand Opening, New Management, New Building, Anniversary, Halloween, New Year. Note: monetizing free/discounted offers is deferred to "Volume III: Money Models" [p174–175].
- **A — Announce Your Avatar**: who you are (and are not) looking for; "as specific as possible, but no more." **Local rule: go hyper-local, not the city — "Not Baltimore but Towson, MD. Not Chicago, but Hinsdale."** Examples: Bee Cave Dentists, Rolling Hills Moms, Brick & Mortar Businesses, Salon Owners, Retired Athletes, Brooklyn Busy Executives [p175].
- **G — Give Them a Goal**: the dream outcome as a word/phrase — event, feeling, experience or outcome; specific and tangible. Examples: Pain Free, Celebrity Smile, 1st Place, Never Out Of Breath, Perfect Product, Grand Slam Offer, Little Black Dress, Double Your Profit, First Client, High Ticket, 7 Figure, 100k [p175].
- **I — Indicate a Time Interval**: expected duration to results. **Compliance warning**: a quantifiable claim (income, weight) with a stated duration implies a guarantee and most ad platforms won't approve it — use duration only where compliance allows, or pair it with a non-claim goal: "$10,000 in 10 days" (not allowed) vs "Make Your First Sale in 10 Days." Examples: minutes, hours, days, weeks, months — "4 Hour", "21 Day", "6 Week", "3 Month" [p175–176].
- **C — Complete With a Container Word**: signals a bundle/system that "can't be held up to a commoditized alternative." Examples: Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive, Incubator, Masterclass, Program, Detox, Experience, Summit, Accelerator, Fast Track, Shortcut, Sprint, Launch, Slingshot, Catapult, Explosion, System, Getaway, Meetup, Transformation, Mastermind, Game Plan, Deep Dive, Workshop, Comeback, Rebirth, Attack, Assault, Reset, Solution, Hack, Cheatcode, Liftoff [p176].
- [S] Pro tips: rhyme (Six-Pack Fast Track, 5-Day Book Print Sprint, Marriage Thrive Deep Dive, 12-Week 2-Putt Shortcut, 12-Month No-Debt Reset) or alliterate (Make Money Masterclass, Change Your Life Challenge, Big Booty Bootcamp, Debt Detox, Real Estate Reset, Life Coach Liftoff); "nice-to-have", don't force [p176].
- [E] Named-offer examples [p177–178]. Wellness: Free Six-Week Lean-By-Halloween Challenge; 88% Off 12-Week Bikini Blueprint; Free 21-Day Mommy Makeover; 60-minute Make Your Friends Jealous Model Hair System; Six-Week Stress-Release Challenge; (Free!) Bend Over Pain Free in 42 Days . . . Healing Fast Track. Doctors: $2,000-Off Celebrity Smile Transformation; Lakeway Moms – $1,500 Off Your Kids Braces; Lakeway Moms – 12 Months To A Perfect Smile ($1000 off for 15 families); Back to School Free Braces Giveaway; Grand Opening Free X-Ray & Treatment – Instant Relief; Back Sore No More! 90 Day Rapid Healing Intensive (81% off!); Tightness? $1 Massage New Client Summer Special. Coaching: 5 Clients in 5 Days Blueprint; 7F Agency 12 Week Intensive; 14 Day Find Your Perfect Product Launch; Fill Your Gym in 30 Days (Free!).
- [S] Testing cadence: run two or three of your best names in a campaign; note the winner; use it as the control to test new names against. "I honestly have no idea why some names win and others do not. So, don't be emotional about it." [p178]
- [S] Pro tip — name every sub-item and bonus in the stack with the same formula [p178].

**FRAMEWORK: WHAT TO CHANGE WHEN OFFERS FATIGUE (order of variation)** [p178–180]
- [S] Change in this order, lightest first; "The lower on the list you go, the more operationally heavy it is":
  1. the creative (images/pictures in ads);
  2. the body copy;
  3. the headline — the "wrapper" of the offer (e.g. Free 6 Week Lean Challenge → Free 6 Week Tone Challenge; Holiday Hangover → New Year New You);
  4. the duration (six weeks → 28 days or eight weeks);
  5. the enhancer (the free/discount component);
  6. the monetization structure — the series of offers and price points ("Book II").
  (Listed as 1–8 in the text; the two example lines are sub-items of step 3.)
- [E] Massage centre: Six-Week Stress Release Challenge → 42-Day Relaxing Holidays Challenge — same core offer [p179].
- [S] "Once you've monetized an offer, rarely should you change it. Just rinse and repeat." Entrepreneurial appetite for change "creates inefficiency and operational drag." Change the machine "only as a last resort and for a darn good reason, especially once you get traction." For initial traction: pick the structure and headline most likely to work and stick with it; if several work, keep the highest-return one; rotating offers is fine only if it creates little operational drag — "multiple 'aces in the hole'" [p179–180].
- [S] Author Note — **Marketing local businesses**: "both easier and harder than national level marketing." Easier because "there is trust in the familiar" — selling in person at higher prices converts a much higher share of leads, so marketing works most of the time. Harder because offers fatigue rapidly: a brick-and-mortar's TAM is its radius, and "the smaller the radius, the faster offers fatigue." Response: vary offers/headlines/creative rapidly; the value stack stays fixed, only its look changes [p180].
- [S] Claim: properly naming the same offer can produce "2x, 3x, or 10x the response rate" [p180].
- Section recap [p181]: value made, services broken into parts and re-bundled; guarantee added so more buy *and consume*; urgency/scarcity for desire; a name that attracts the right prospects and repels the wrong ones, "all while containing a big promise everyone can understand."
