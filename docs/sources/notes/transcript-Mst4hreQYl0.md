### 0. Hook and premise (00:00:00–00:00:55)
- [S] Promise: more leads, and more sales from those leads, "without increasing any of your advertising".
- [E] Credential: owns acquisition.com, a portfolio that did over $250M aggregate revenue "last year".

### 1. Ashley and the definition of a lead magnet (00:00:53–00:02:30)
- [E] A deep dive with Ashley, a fashion personal stylist: her site had the classic "book a call" / "ask for a quote" CTA.
- [S] Condition: a book-a-call CTA "only works if you already have an informed audience" — people who know you and have already got value from you. If your traffic is smaller or colder, you need a better reason for them to hand over contact information.
- [S] Reframe: the sole objective of "submit for a quote / book a call" is lead capture (plus automatic booking if you have a calendar). So ask: is there anything that would raise the likelihood of capturing the lead, provided the capture method still signals interest in buying?
- [S] Definition: a **mini offer that is a complete solution to a narrow problem**, typically low cost or free, to see who raises their hand. Solving that narrow problem **reveals another problem that is solved by your core offer**. Leads interested in a low-cost or free offer are more likely to buy a related higher-cost offer later. (Author's own cross-reference: written on "page 31 in the Leads book".)
- [I] Same definition as Leads (2023) ch. 2 and as transcript 7qY7gBMWOB4 §7 (same Ashley example).

### 2. The arithmetic of "help first" (00:02:30–00:03:41)
- [S] Illustration: 100 people told "come buy my thing" → maybe 1 buys. The same 100 told "you don't have to buy anything, I just want to help" and then asked afterwards → "three to five times" as many buyers.
- [S] You are not getting more traffic — you convert a higher percentage of it; no extra marketing spend; the improvement "drops to your bottom line".

### 3. Origin story: webinar vs free case study (00:03:41–00:05:14)
- [E] April 2016: paid $25,000 to join a group; everyone told him to run a webinar; it did not work — "I didn't have the skill at the time to do one".
- [E] Saw an ad in his feed: "free case study on how I spent $1 and made $120,000 in a weekend" — just a screen recording of how the guy did it. He swapped his webinar for a video headlined "Free case study: how we added 213 members to a gym and $112,000 in San Diego" (a small gym). The next morning Leila's calendar was full.
- [S] Lesson: a webinar can be perceived as a lead magnet depending on positioning, but the more advanced the audience (business owners, sophisticated buyers), the more they recognise it as a sales pitch and the less it converts. "Consume it on your own time, let me show you what I did" got far more people to exchange contact info.

### 4. Test the packaging more than the contents (00:05:14–00:05:33)
- [S] If a lead magnet fails, "it doesn't mean lead magnets don't work, it means that lead magnet didn't work" — as with an ad headline.
- [S] He is "adamant" about testing the wrapping/packaging more than the contents: changing only the headline can change how many people want it by 2×, 3×, 10×. Condition: the contents must clearly solve a problem; then it is about packaging.

### 5. Why lead magnets work; the three types in brief (00:05:33–00:07:20)
- [S] Costco samplers: reciprocity is a factor, but the main reason people buy after tasting is "that's good, maybe I'll have more".
- [S] Three types: (1) a **sample or trial** — a small piece of something much bigger; (2) **one step in a multi-step process** — [E] Ashley: first you need your colours, then you have new problems (tops, bottoms, formal vs informal); (3) an **assessment / revealing a problem** — [E] a free website speed test; the core offer is fixing it. Any of the three, or combinations, work.
- [S] The shift: from "buy my thing" to "do you want this free thing?" → after consumption "did you like the free thing? then you'll love the paid thing" — and now you hold their contact info.
- [S] Mechanism: "when someone pays with time now, they're more likely to pay with money later". Get an easier, lower-barrier investment first to raise the likelihood of a larger investment later.

### 6. Objections: "they don't work", freebie seekers, giving away too much (00:07:20–00:10:12)
- [S] "Lead magnets don't work" → "bad lead magnets don't work". When you start out "you just don't know that you suck", so you blame the tactic. Webinars work; he lacked the skill. The lead magnet was an easier skill for him: screen-record a campaign and its results; then call the opt-in: "how'd you like that? want me to do it at your place? risk-free — if I don't perform you don't pay."
- [S] Stage note: if you do not have a lead magnet you are making less money than you could because you are getting fewer leads than you could.
- [S] "I don't want freebie seekers / tire kickers" → you do not have to give the lead magnet to everyone: add a dropdown that qualifies; route qualified people to the good thing and unqualified people to something they might qualify for.
- [S] "I don't want to give away too much value" — "a real thing, sort of". Rule: do **not** solve with the free thing the problem your core product solves; **do** solve a problem that leads to the core issue. The person must remain deprived of what you sell, and that deprivation should be triggered by solving the first issue.
- [S] Restaurant analogy: after a big entrée, "another entrée?" is refused — not because it was bad but because the need is satisfied; dessert is still unsatisfied. In business, the "dessert" should be significantly more expensive than the "entrée", which you give free or at cost.
- [S] Principle: sell at the point of greatest deprivation. Sell water to the thirsty; the moment after they drink says nothing about your water — they are simply not thirsty — but they may now want food.

### 7. Type 1 in depth — reveal a problem (00:10:12–00:12:53)
- [S] His personal favourite; "if you ever have the opportunity to build one of these… exceptional". It literally creates deprivation: "here's a problem you didn't know existed, or knew but let me show how bad it is" — widening the gap between where they are and where they could be.
- [S] Bonus: also show what it looks like solved and the delta.
- [E] B2B example: free website speed test → "for every second of load time you lose 3% of conversion; we can take you from 9 seconds to 3 → an 18% increase; what could you do with 18% more business? Relative to your revenue, 18% versus what I charge for websites is nothing — how soon do you want me to start?"
- [S] Condition: works best for problems that **get worse while waiting** — posture analysis, termite inspection (already active), financial audits (back taxes, cash flow) — because urgency is built in; deprivation increases every second after they learn of it.
- [E] ALAN's highest-converting ad of all time: "four reasons why you'll never have a million-dollar agency", aimed at small SMB lead-gen agencies; readers recognised all four in themselves.
- [S] Rule: be **very specific on the negatives**, which lets you be **vaguer on the positives** — more compliant marketing and more realistic expectations. If you nail all their problems and say "I can help with that", they believe you; no need to inflate promises.

### 8. Type 2 in depth — free trial / sample (00:12:53–00:15:10)
- [S] The classic taste test / try before you buy / fitting room; the lowest barrier, hence common.
- [S] Design rule: give them the thing with a full loop to the end of the trial, so that **removing it creates deprivation** — that is what converts (his crack-cocaine analogy: no deprivation until they have tried it and it is taken away).
- [S] Limit some aspect: **number of uses, quantity, time**, or a combination ("x-day trial", "this many hits"); combinations can be more compelling.
- [E] Gym Launch: customers bought the monetisation system (how to make the gym more profitable) and got agency (lead-gen) services free for **four months**. After four months of leads arriving, "I still want leads" → "now you can pay for them" — a built-in back-end upsell created by deprivation.

### 9. Type 3 in depth — one step of many (00:15:10–00:15:56)
- [S] One of his personal favourites; **particularly effective for more complex products and services**.
- [E] Multiple coats of paint on a garage: sell the first, they need the rest. Med-spa laser hair removal: 6–8 sessions needed; one session is "worthless on its own" — sell one, upsell the rest. A comprehensive course: give away the first two videos.

### 10. Four ways to deliver a lead magnet (00:15:56–00:22:30)
- [S] **(1) Software / tools** — something that does a job: spreadsheets that calculate, assessment tools, templates, actual software. [E] Neil Patel's URL tool that assesses your site and collects your info. Tools can fulfil any of the three types (reveal a problem, free trial, one step of many).
- [S] **(2) Information** — "costs nothing", "infinitely scalable", tremendous value, creates deprivation, "basically no operational drag". Examples: mini courses, guides, expert interviews, static templates. [E] His scaling roadmap = tool + information: you go through the assessment, it outputs information; built "after studying all the businesses we looked at for 200 plus hours"; CTA behind it: book a one-on-one call with his team, then possibly an invitation to HQ. "These are not static concepts, you can combine them."
- [S] **(3) Services** — "people sleep on this so hard": do work for free, create goodwill. Objection "freebie seekers" → **only do the free work for qualified people**. Perfect lead: has the budget, the authority to decide, clearly needs it, wants to act now; give the free service only to people who agree to those up front; giving it to someone broke, indecisive, without need and unsure of timing is "a terrible waste of your time". Formats: free audits with some implementation, same-day service delivery, any done-for-you component.
- [S] Service lead-magnet math: 1 hour of labour, $25 hard cost, market value $250+ ("$250 for free where a real person does work is fairly compelling"). If 1 in 4 recipients buys, CAC = $25 × 4 = $100. Would you pay $100 for a customer? "Provided I'm making a lot more than $100 — probably."
- [E] His very first business: trained people for free for a year ("the free training project") to collect testimonials; then showed the testimonials and more people came, paying. After the year: "I have too much demand, do you want to pay me now?" — almost all said yes. "If you get the right people they will continue to stay and pay, provided you do a good job."
- [S] **(4) Physical products** — [E] his books are lead magnets ("obviously incredibly valuable"); after you fix your offer and grow, you want help to speed it up → call us; after you go from $1M to $50M, invest/partner → "a very long game". Combine categories freely (physical + information; software + information).
- [E] Conference example: give a hat or "CEO" shirt to anyone who proves they are a CEO → an incredibly qualified list. Cost $3–4 per shirt; even at 1 in 20 converting, "$100 for a CEO feels like a good idea". Ask: what would the type of person I want actually want, and can I just give it to them?

### 11. Naming the lead magnet (00:22:30–00:25:35)
- [S] "So slept on… people underestimate the value of this by a mile." The name determines engagement rate "more than anything else". The real secret: **ask your audience**.
- [E] His first gym: "Big Booty Boot Camp" — the same programme as a "six-week deadlift and squat seminar" (deadlift, squat, hip thrust for six weeks) but the seminar name would not convert with his primary female audience. "I'm not going to advertise the vehicle, I'm going to advertise the result."
- [S] Different names attract different people ("tight and toned glutes" vs "Bubble Butt Boot Camp"); test names to find the one that attracts the most leads and ideally the highest-quality leads.
- [S] Testing ladder: (1) get very clear on the avatar, run small ad tests comparing headlines; (2) no ad budget → poll your audience in stories, pre-filtering responders ("if you're not a business owner, please don't answer"); (3) no audience → text people and ask "which one do you want?", or a post asking for "A or B" in comments; combine two or three methods for a directional answer.
- [E] "$100M Leads": split-tested six title options (including "advertising", "promotion", "marketing"), the cover image, and the sub-headlines; results shown inside the book. Reasoning: "if I'm going to spend two years writing the book, I can spend two days testing the headline", which drives short-term sales more than anything. Caveat: long term, the content between the covers decides, because in books word of mouth is "the only thing that matters"; anyone can launch a book, few launch one that keeps selling.

### 12. Naming conventions that work (00:25:35–00:26:45)
- [S] (1) **Number + outcome + time frame** — e.g. "three emails that turn cold leads into clients in 24 hours".
- [S] (2) **How to [good thing] without [bad thing] / even if [greatest insecurity]** — e.g. "how to build a funnel without hiring a copywriter, even if you've never done it before / it's your first time".
- [S] (3) **[Adjective] + [thing]** — e.g. "the lazy funnel template that converts like crazy".
- [S] (4) **X mistakes** — "four mistakes keeping your business under a million dollars a year"; "three mistakes you're probably making"; "five ad-copy mistakes killing your conversion rate"; general form: X mistakes that prevent [good thing] / are killing [metric].

### 13. Making money with it: the call to action (00:26:45–00:30:55)
- [S] Where most people fail: they build a great lead magnet and forget the CTA — and even those who include one do not repeat it. Stat cited (no source): the salespeople who ask the most times get the most deals; in marketing "ask as many times as you can".
- [S] Constraint: asking constantly makes people stop giving you the opportunity. Optimise **ability to ask × number of asks**: keep providing value between asks; raise "value per second" so people accept the ask because they just got value and expect more.
- [S] **CTA formula**: (1) **clear, not clever** — a direct instruction; (2) the **exact next action**; (3) a **reason to do it now**. Scarcity and/or urgency can be used in any CTA — "great if you've got it" — but at minimum make the CTA.
- [E] His books end every chapter with a CTA ("free gift: everything I learned from XYZ") → acquisition.com → monthly workshop → one step closer to becoming a portfolio company: "walking them down the process".
- [S] Reason-to-act-now: urgency or scarcity is just an additional good reason; **any reason beats no reason**. Cites the university line-cutting study: "can I cut?" → no; "because I'm late for class" → yes; "because I have a dog" → still yes. Ideally the reason makes sense, but a weak reason still beats none. Examples: one-year business anniversary, "my daughter lost her first tooth", National Dog Day.
- [S] Service businesses ("78% of businesses are service") have real capacity limits — you cannot take 1,000 customers tomorrow. [E] Fitness coach with limited spots: "sign up now or wait until the next spots open". If four more customers fill you up, say so: prospects assume they know how big you are and they do not. Small business → leverage real constraints as scarcity. Big business with capacity → rolling cohorts: "we can only start this many per week / to be in this group, do it this Friday".

### 14. Recap (00:30:55–00:31:29)
- [S] Give value first by revealing a problem, giving a free trial, or giving one step of many; deliver via software, information, services, physical products or combinations; name it compellingly (conventions above, or ask the audience); embed CTAs **within, before and after** the lead magnet.
